Safety Practices

The hazards that are specific to this work — open CT secondaries, station DC, arc flash, induced voltage, and stored energy — and the habits that keep them from mattering

☰The rules, stated plainly

Non-negotiables

  • Never open an energized CT secondary. Short it first, verify the short, then open. This is the hazard most specific to protection work and the one least understood by people from other trades.
  • Assume every conductor is energized until you have proven otherwise — with a tester you have just proven on a known source.
  • Station DC is not “low voltage” in the sense that matters. A battery is a very low impedance source; a dropped tool across the terminals is a several-thousand-ampere event.
  • Arc flash is about energy, not voltage. The incident-energy label on the panel is the number that decides what you wear and whether you open the door at all.
  • Backfeed is real. A de-energized circuit can be energized from a DER, a station service transformer, a closed tie, or a customer generator.
  • If you did not put the lock on, you do not own the clearance. Your own lock, your own tag, every time.

1Open CT secondaries

A current transformer is a current source. The primary conductor determines the secondary current, and the CT will develop whatever voltage it takes to push that current through whatever is connected. Interrupt the circuit and “whatever it takes” becomes a very large number.

Test switch mechanics — make-before-break on current blades, break-before-make on potential blades — are covered in Control Wiring & Terminations. (Animated in the browser.)

The mirror-image hazard: shorting a PT secondary. A PT is a voltage source, so shorting it is a bolted fault limited only by its fuse. Current blades short; potential blades must never. Know which one your hand is on.

2Arc flash

  • The label is the instruction. Incident energy at the working distance, the arc-flash boundary, and the required PPE category are all on it. If there is no label, the equipment has not been studied and you should not be opening it.
  • Energy scales with clearing time, so anything that speeds up protection lowers the hazard — maintenance-mode settings, bus blocking, arc-flash detection. See Arc-Flash Detection.
  • Racking and switching are the high-risk operations. Remote racking and remote operation remove the person from the boundary entirely, which beats any amount of PPE.
  • De-energized is the goal. Every energized-work justification should be genuinely examined; “it would be inconvenient to take an outage” is not one.
  • Your settings changes change the label. Adding coordination delay raises incident energy. Feed protection changes back to whoever maintains the study — this is a real and frequently missed obligation.

3Station DC

  • Very low source impedance. A wrench across the battery terminals produces thousands of amperes and an explosive arc. Insulated tools, no jewelry, one hand where practical.
  • DC arcs do not self-extinguish. There is no current zero. Open circuits at the device designed for it, never at a terminal block.
  • Hydrogen. Flooded batteries generate hydrogen while charging. Ventilation stays on; no ignition sources in the battery room.
  • Electrolyte. Face and eye protection, appropriate gloves, and know where the eyewash is before you need it.
  • A DC ground is a live hazard, not a nuisance alarm — a second ground can trip or block a breaker. See Station DC Systems.
  • Your test equipment can create a ground. Grounded-chassis instruments on a floating DC system are a common cause of “a ground appeared while you were on site.”

5Clearances and lockout

  1. Identify every source of energy — electrical, mechanical, stored, and pneumatic or hydraulic.
  2. Isolate with a device capable of isolation, and open it visibly where the design provides for that.
  3. Lock and tag with your own lock and your own tag. Group lockboxes exist for multi-person work; use them properly.
  4. Test for absence of voltage with an instrument you have proven on a known live source immediately before and immediately after — live, dead, live.
  5. Ground where required. Personal protective grounds are what protect against re-energization and induced voltage, not the isolation itself.
  6. Verify with the operator what has actually been done, in the words of the clearance, before you start.
  7. Restore in reverse, deliberately, with the same rigor — grounds removed and counted, locks removed by their owners, and the operator informed.
The most dangerous moment is restoration. Everyone is tired, the work is “done,” and the pressure is to finish. This is precisely when a shorting screw stays in, a ground stays on, or a lock is cut because the owner has gone home. Slow down for the last thirty minutes.

The restoration half of this list is where the mistakes happen, because everyone is tired and the work feels finished. (Animated in the browser.)

6Working on in-service protection

Much automation work happens on live equipment where the protection must stay in service, or where taking it out has to be explicit and temporary.

  • Know what you are removing from service, and tell someone. Isolating a trip circuit for testing is a protection outage. It should be logged, time-bounded, and known to operations.
  • Use the relay's own facilities where possible — test modes, disabled outputs, settings groups — rather than physically isolating, so the state is visible and recorded.
  • One protection out at a time. Primary and backup out simultaneously is an unprotected circuit.
  • Write down every isolation as you make it and check the list before you leave. This list and the shorting-jumper count are the two most important pieces of paper on the job.
  • Beware the accidental trip. Injecting into a relay whose outputs are still connected trips the breaker. Confirm isolation before injecting, every time.
  • Announce before you operate anything. A breaker that operates unexpectedly is a hazard to whoever is working on it elsewhere.

7Habits

Say what you are about to doOut loud, to whoever is with you. “I am about to open the current blade on phase B” is the sentence that gets corrected before it becomes an incident.
Prove your testerLive, dead, live. On a known source, every time, not once at the start of the week.
Count thingsJumpers in, jumpers out. Grounds on, grounds off. Locks on, locks off. Out loud, with a second person.
Photograph before you disturbSwitch positions, terminal landings, jumper placement. Ten seconds now, an hour saved later.
Stop when surprisedA surprise means your model is wrong. Continuing with a wrong model is how incidents are built.
Refuse the rushed job“We just need it back by six” is not a safety plan. The outage can be extended; the injury cannot be undone.

8Gotchas

  • Assuming a test switch shorted correctly. Blades corrode. Verify with a clamp on the shorted path.
  • Meggering with electronics connected. Destroys relay inputs, RTAC I/O, and surge suppressors. Build the disconnection into the written procedure.
  • Confusing current and potential blades. They look similar and behave oppositely.
  • Trusting a label on an unlabeled-in-practice panel. Trace it. Panels get modified and labels do not.
  • Working from a drawing that is out of date. Verify before you rely, and red-line what you find — see One-Lines & Schematics.
  • Forgetting the second source. Station service, control power transformers, DER, and closed ties.
  • PPE selected by habit rather than by label. The study exists; read it.
  • The unlabeled temporary jumper. If it is temporary, it is labeled and it is on the list. Otherwise it is permanent and nobody knows why.
