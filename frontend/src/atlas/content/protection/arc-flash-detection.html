☰The rules, stated plainly

Arc-flash detection

  • Incident energy is current times time. You usually cannot change the current, so the only lever is clearing time — and arc-flash detection exists to make that time very small.
  • Light is faster than current filtering. The arc-flash overcurrent elements work on raw A/D samples and need only two consecutive samples above threshold, so the current decision is made in a fraction of a cycle rather than a full one.
  • Trip on light AND current. Light alone is fast but insecure; current alone is what you already have. The combination is both fast and secure, and it is the recommended scheme.
  • Sensors must see the arc, and must not see the sun. Direct sunlight can exceed 20,000 lux — comparable to an arc — so do not install arc-flash sensors where sunlight can reach them.
  • The output contact matters. Fast high-current interrupting outputs operate roughly 8 ms faster than standard outputs — but only in non-failsafe mode — and the output must be listed in AOUTSLOT so its logic is processed every 1/16 cycle instead of every 1/4 cycle.
  • Detection is one of several tools. Maintenance-mode settings groups, bus blocking schemes, and remote racking all reduce exposure too, and they are complementary.

1Why clearing time is the whole game

An arcing fault in switchgear releases energy into the surrounding air as heat, pressure, and molten metal. The energy a worker at a given distance receives — incident energy — scales roughly with the arcing current and directly with how long the arc burns. Halve the clearing time and you halve the incident energy.

Detection removes the relay's decision time from the total. What remains is the breaker, and that sets the floor. (Animated in the browser.)

2The sensors

Arc-flash light sensors are rugged flexible optical assemblies that plug into the relay's arc-flash input card with a dual V-pin latch. A black-jacketed zipcord duplex fiber — two fiber leads molded together — runs from the card to the detection area, where it connects to the clear light-sensing portion.

Point sensors

  • A small collector lens aimed at a specific area — a breaker compartment, a cable termination cubicle.
  • The black-jacketed fiber run is limited to 35 metres (about 115 ft).
  • Two variants: a standard point sensor mounted inside the switchgear, and a window point sensor mounted outside — useful where the interior is hard to access.

Fiber loop sensors

  • A length of clear-jacketed fiber routed as a loop through a larger area — a bus compartment, a whole cubicle row.
  • The clear-jacketed loop portion can run up to 50 metres, with black-jacketed sections up to 30 metres, and the total fiber length of the sensor must be under 70 metres.
  • Better coverage, less precise location — you learn that somewhere in the loop's area an arc occurred.
Placement is the design. A sensor that cannot see the arc detects nothing, and a sensor that can see daylight is a nuisance trip waiting for a sunny afternoon. Point sensors in compartments where you want to know exactly which one; loop sensors for open bus areas. Never where direct sunlight can reach.

3The elements, and how to set them

ElementWhat it isSetting guidance
50PAF — arc-flash phase overcurrentWorks on raw A/D samples; requires two consecutive samples above threshold to declare an overcurrent conditionSet 50PAFP at at least twice the expected maximum load. Fast sample-based detectors are susceptible to harmonics, so the margin is there to prevent unintended pickup
50NAF — arc-flash neutral overcurrentSame fast sample-based detection, on the neutral quantitySet 50NAFP like a standard neutral overcurrent element plus margin for harmonics — about half the available ground-fault current, but never below the natural ground current produced by load or system unbalance
TOL — time-overlightOne inverse TOL element per light sensor input. Two settings per element: the sensor type (none, point, or fiber loop) and the pickup in percent of A/D converter rangeSet TOLnP at the lowest level above the highest expected ambient light at that sensor. A properly placed sensor reads 100% during a real arc-flash event, so there is enormous headroom

Measuring the ambient

The recommended way to establish the TOL pickup is to expose each sensor to its worst-case ambient light and read the actual value, using the MET L command (or Meter → Light Intensity from the front panel):

Ambient in the low single digits of percent, an arc at 100% — the separation is large, which is why a small margin above measured ambient is sufficient as long as the trip is also supervised by current.

The overcurrent elements will pick up briefly during inrush. That is expected and harmless, because the light supervision keeps the relay from declaring an arc-flash condition. This is the practical reason the AND scheme is preferred: each element covers the other's false-positive mode.

4The trip logic

Multiple sensors covering one section are simply OR'd together in the TOL term. (Animated in the browser.)

Two speed details that are easy to miss

  • Use the fast high-current interrupting outputs for the arc-flash trip. They operate about 8 ms faster than standard outputs — but that advantage exists only in non-failsafe mode. Configure a fast output in failsafe mode and it is no quicker than a standard one.
  • Include the output in AOUTSLOT. Doing so causes that output's logic to be processed every 1/16 of a cycle instead of the relay's typical 1/4-cycle interval. On a 60 Hz system that is roughly a 3 ms saving, for free.
The unsupervised variant exists, and is normally not recommended. OUTxxx = TOLn OR TRIP trips on light alone. It is marginally faster and considerably less secure, since bright light by itself will trip the breaker. Reserve it for genuinely noncritical loads, and understand exactly what will trip them.

5Commissioning

  1. Current only. With a standard test set, apply currents below 50PAFP and 50NAFP, then ramp up slowly and verify the 50PAF and 50NAF Relay Word bits assert. Confirm the relay does not trip on arc-flash logic with current alone.
  2. Light only. Expose each sensor to a light source above its TOLnP pickup and verify the corresponding TOL bit asserts — and again that no trip occurs with light alone.
  3. Both together. Apply current above pickup and light above pickup simultaneously; the relay should trip, and the event report should show both conditions.
  4. Verify sensor-to-compartment mapping. Illuminate one sensor at a time and confirm the relay identifies the right one. Mislabeled sensors give you a fast trip and no idea where the arc was.
  5. Record the ambient readings from MET L as your as-commissioned baseline, so a future drift (a new light fixture, a door left open) is detectable.
  6. Check the output configuration: the trip output is a fast high-current interrupting contact, it is in non-failsafe mode, and it is listed in AOUTSLOT.

6The other levers on incident energy

Maintenance-mode settings group A second settings group with a fast, low-set instantaneous, selected only while people are working nearby. Trades selectivity for speed, deliberately and temporarily. Needs a clear indication and a SCADA point so nobody leaves it in.
Bus blocking / zone-selective interlocking Feeder relays send a fast block to the main when they see the fault; absent a block, the main trips immediately. Gives near-instantaneous bus protection without sacrificing feeder selectivity.
Bus differential Where a dedicated 87B exists it clears bus faults in under a cycle with no coordination delay at all — see Bus Protection.
Remote racking and remote operation Moves the person out of the arc-flash boundary entirely. The most effective mitigation is not being there.
Arc-quenching / crowbar devices Deliberately create a bolted fault to collapse the arc voltage, transferring energy away from the arc. Fast, expensive, and independent of breaker time.
Study, label, and PPE The incident-energy study and the equipment label are what make every other decision defensible — and they must be updated when protection settings change.
Changing protection settings changes the arc-flash label. If you make a coordination change that adds delay, the incident energy at that equipment went up and the label is now wrong. Feed protection changes back to whoever owns the arc-flash study — this is a real and frequently missed obligation.

7Which relays, and gotchas

SEL relays that combine light sensing with fast overcurrent for arc-flash detection include the SEL-751 and SEL-751A feeder relays, the SEL-710-5 motor relay, the SEL-851 feeder relay, and the SEL-849 motor management relay. The SEL-849 has a built-in fiber-optic arc-flash detector input; the SEL-710-5 offers four- or eight-channel arc-flash detector cards as options.

  • Sunlight. Above about 20,000 lux, direct sun looks like an arc. Never place sensors where sun can reach them, including through a door that is opened for maintenance.
  • Fiber length limits are hard limits. 35 m for a point sensor's black fiber; 30 m black plus 50 m clear with a 70 m total for a loop. Exceeding them costs sensitivity quietly.
  • Failsafe mode silently removes the speed benefit of the fast output. Check the mode, not just the contact type.
  • Forgetting AOUTSLOT leaves a few milliseconds on the table for no reason.
  • Setting 50PAFP from load rather than from twice load risks harmonic pickup on the sample-based detector.
  • Omitting the arc-flash terms from TR means no trip seal-in and no event report — you get the trip but lose the evidence.
  • Sensor damage during construction. Clear fiber is not rugged cable. Inspect and re-run MET L after any switchgear work.
  • Arc-flash detection does not replace the study. It lowers the number; somebody still has to calculate and label it.
