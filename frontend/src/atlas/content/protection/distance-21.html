Distance Protection (21)

V/I becomes ohms, ohms become distance — mho and quad zones, stepped timers, pilot schemes, and everything that keeps a 21 honest on SEL-311C-class relays

☰The rules, stated plainly

  • A distance (21) element divides measured voltage by measured current and compares the result — an apparent impedance — against a reach set in Ω secondary. Because line impedance is proportional to length, ohms are distance.
  • Zone 1 underreaches (typ. 80–90% of the line) and trips with no intentional delay. Zone 2 overreaches (typ. 125%) and waits out its timer. Zone 3 usually looks backward to feed pilot and current-reversal logic.
  • The element is only as good as its inputs: LOP blocks it when PTs die, load encroachment keeps heavy load from looking like a three-phase fault, and k0 is what makes ground zones read line ohms at all.
  • A pilot channel (POTT / DCB / DCUB) turns end-zone Zone-2-time clearing into channel-time clearing at both ends.

1Ohms as distance

The relay measures the voltage drop between its PTs and the fault, and the current through its CTs, and computes the impedance between itself and the fault. For a bolted fault at 60% of the line, the phase elements see roughly 0.6 × Zline. Every reach is therefore just a fraction of the line impedance, entered in secondary ohms:

Ω primary × (CTR / PTR) = Ω secondary. On the SEL-311C the line is described by Z1MAG/Z1ANG (positive-sequence) and Z0MAG/Z0ANG (zero-sequence), all in Ω secondary, plus a unitless line length LL — these feed the distance elements, the fault locator, and (with E32 = AUTO) the automatic directional-element settings.

The catch driving the rest of this page: the relay measures only at its own terminal. Anything that distorts V/I — infeed from another terminal, fault resistance, load flow, a dying PT fuse, a CCVT transient — moves the measured point on the R-X plane without moving the fault.

2Zones, reaches, and timers (SEL-311C)

The SEL-311C provides four independent zones each of mho phase (E21P, reaches Z1P–Z4P), mho ground (E21MG, Z1MG–Z4MG), and quadrilateral ground (E21XG, reactance XG1–XG4 with resistance blinders RG1–RG4). Zones 1 and 2 are fixed forward; Zones 3 and 4 are settable forward or reverse (DIR3/DIR4). Reach range: 0.05–64 Ω sec in 0.01 Ω steps (5 A nominal). Each zone is supervised by fault-detector overcurrent pickups (50PP1–4 phase, 50L1–4/50GZ1–4 ground).

3The characteristics on the R-X plane

A mho characteristic is a circle passing through the origin with its diameter along the line angle, out to the reach setting. The relay implements it as an angle test between a line-drop-compensated voltage (Z·I − V) and a polarizing reference: the balance point is 0 = Re[(Z·I − V) · Vmem*]. Inside the circle the test operates; outside it restrains. Everything in one picture:

R-X plane: the measured impedance sits in the load region until a fault snaps it onto the line. Inside Z1 → instantaneous; between the Z1 and Z2 reaches → the Z2 timer must run out first. ▶ Animated in the HTML edition.

Polarization and memory — why the circle is bigger than it looks

The 311C polarizes both mho phase and mho ground elements with memorized positive-sequence voltage (V1mem). Memory keeps a reference alive through close-in faults where the measured voltage collapses to zero, and it expands the mho characteristic back toward the source impedance — improving resistive coverage for forward faults while staying secure. Relay Word bit VPOLV supervises every distance element and asserts only while the memorized polarizing voltage exceeds 1 V; the DCB logic even latches its block-send for a zero-voltage three-phase fault that outlives the memory. Faulted-phase selection (FIDS) compares the angle between I0 and I2 and enables only the right loops — an A-G fault asserts FSA, enabling AG and CB elements and blocking BG, CG, AB, CA.

Quadrilateral ground and k0

  • Quadrilateral ground zones are a box instead of a circle: a reactance line (reach XGn) capped by resistance blinders (±RGn, 0.05–50 Ω sec) — deliberately wide resistive coverage for high-resistance ground faults on short lines. The reactance line is polarized by negative-sequence current (or zero-sequence, advanced setting XGPOL), and tilted by TANG (−40° to +40°, −3° when advanced settings are off) to fight over/underreach on non-homogeneous systems.
  • Zero-sequence compensation: a ground loop measures VΦ/(IΦ + k0·IR) with k0 = (Z0 − Z1)/(3·Z1), entered as magnitude k0M1 (0–6) and angle k0A1 (±180°) from the line's Z1/Z0 data. Zones 2–4 get their own k0 only with EADVS = Y; otherwise they inherit Zone 1's. Wrong k0 = every ground zone reaches the wrong distance.
  • Compensator distance (a "C" suffix on E21P, e.g. 3C) swaps the phase elements for a compensator principle that reaches correctly through delta-wye transformer banks; it cannot run at the same time as positive-sequence-polarized phase distance.

4Time-stepped zones — and their built-in flaw

Stepped zones exist so a stand-alone relay never overtrips for the next line: Zone 1 stops short of the remote bus because reach accuracy (±5% at line angle for 30 ≤ SIR ≤ 60, ±3% below) plus CT/PT error could otherwise carry it past; Zone 2 covers the rest of the line but must wait long enough for the remote Zone 1 and breaker to clear an external fault first. The price: a fault in the last ~20% of the line is cleared at one end in a cycle and at the other only after the full Zone 2 delay — slow clearing, longer arc time, and reclosing that can't be trusted until both ends are open. 5Communications-assisted (pilot) schemes

The 311C builds six schemes in, no external coordination devices: DTT and DUTT (via the DTT trip equation), and four enabled by ECOMM = POTT (covers POTT and PUTT), DCUB1/DCUB2, or DCB. All work on two- or three-terminal lines. The pilot trip elements go in TRCOMM — typically M2P + Z2G — and all schemes want Zone 3 set reverse (DIR3 = R). SEL's guidance: run the scheme over Mirrored Bits (see the Mirrored Bits page in atlas for the channel itself) — POTT on a reliable, noise-free channel like dedicated fiber; DCUB when the channel is less than perfect but failures won't coincide with external faults.

6Three-terminal lines: the infeed problem (AG2000-12)

Add a tapped third terminal and the relay's ohms stop being distance. For a fault beyond the tap, the tap's current contribution raises the voltage drop on the far segment without flowing through the local CTs, so the local relay sees more ohms than the wire: in the guide's example (1 Ω per leg, equal infeed) the actual impedance to the fault is 2 Ω but the apparent impedance is 3 Ω. With outfeed at the tap the same fault measures only 1.5 Ω — and worse, the outfeed terminal can see the internal fault as external and key blocking.

  • Zone 1: set on actual impedance, never apparent. 80–90% of the true line impedance to the nearest terminal — if you set it on the inflated apparent value, opening the infeed breaker makes Zone 1 overreach the remote bus. If outfeed is possible, the reach may need to shrink further.
  • Zone 2: at least 125% of the apparent impedance for a remote-end fault with infeed in — otherwise the permissive element can't see the far end and the pilot scheme never keys. Adding a ground directional overcurrent element to TRCOMM buys sensitivity.
  • A transformer tap with a grounded-wye line-side winding contributes no positive/negative-sequence current but plenty of zero-sequence — phase elements are unaffected, ground elements still see infeed.
  • Directional elements: E32 = AUTO is for two-terminal lines; on three terminals set E32 = Y and enter thresholds manually (guide values: Z2F = −0.30, Z2R = 0.30, Z0F = −0.30, Z0R = 0.30 Ω, 5 A).
  • POTT needs both permissions (PT1 = IN104 * IN106), so the stock echo logic fails when two terminals are weak — only custom SELOGIC echo (or accepting sequential Zone-2-time clearing) covers that case. DCB and POTT-with-echo clear sequentially instead: the strong terminal trips, its infeed disappears, and the remaining terminals then see the fault — slower than simultaneous, but with no intentional delay.

7Supervision that keeps a 21 honest

Loss of potential — LOP

A blown PT fuse looks like a close-in fault. The 311C declares LOP on a 10% drop in V1 with no corresponding change in I1 or I0; after 15 cycles it latches, resetting only when all phase voltages exceed 40 V, V0 < 5 V, and V2 < 15% of V1. With ELOP = Y or Y1, ILOP disables all distance elements and every voltage-polarized directional; Y additionally lets forward-set overcurrent elements go nondirectional as fallback protection, and Y1 also blocks POTT keying and echo. The logic needs a closed breaker (3PO deasserted); for PT-transfer / breaker-and-a-half throw-over schemes use EBBPT = Y so busbar-protection logic can assert LOP with the breaker open.

Load encroachment

Heavy balanced load is a low, resistive positive-sequence impedance that can wander into a long Zone 3/4. ELOAD = Y carves load regions out of the R-X plane: ZLF/ZLR minimum load impedances with angle limits PLAF/NLAF/PLAR/NLAR, active once I1 > 0.1 × INOM. Inside them ZLOAD asserts and blocks the positive-sequence directional element, so phase distance M1P–M4P cannot operate on load; put !ZLOAD in phase-overcurrent torque control too. Ground elements don't need it — balanced load makes no residual.

CCVT transients & short lines (SIR)

On a short line behind a weak source (SIR = Zsource/Zline high), the fault voltage is tiny and a CCVT's transient can fake a Zone 1 overreach. With ECCVT = Y the relay detects high SIR during a Zone 1 fault and holds the trip up to 1.5 cycles until the distance calculation is smooth — no user tuning. SEL recommends it for SIR > 5 or CCVTs with active ferroresonance suppression. Reach accuracy itself degrades with SIR: ±3% below SIR 30, ±5% for SIR 30–60; operating time also stretches at high SIR (see the 311C speed curves).

Out-of-step blocking

A power swing walks the apparent impedance slowly into the zones. The 311C's OOS logic (EOOS = Y) times the trajectory between two concentric polygons (Zones 6 → 5, reaches X1T5/6, R1R5/6): slower than OSBD = swing → OSB1–OSB4 block whichever zones you select (OOSB1–4). Out-of-step tripping is separate (EOOST = I/O, delay OSTD). Not usable when Z1ANG < 45°.

8Gotchas & fine print

  • k0 wrong → every ground reach wrong. It comes straight from Z1/Z0 line data; a stale zero-sequence model (mutual coupling, reconductoring) shifts real reach without touching the setting sheet.
  • Ω secondary, always. A CTR or PTR entered wrong scales every zone by the same silent factor — the classic "relay reached one bus too far" root cause. Check: Ω primary × CTR/PTR.
  • Zone 1 margin is not pessimism. ±5% element accuracy at high SIR, plus transient overreach <5%, plus instrument-transformer error is exactly why 80–90% — and why infeed-inflated "apparent" ohms must never be used to set it (Section 6).
  • Fault resistance is invisible ohms. A resistive ground fault adds R the mho circle may not cover — that's what quadrilateral ground zones and their RGn blinders are for.
  • Pilot timers are a matched set. Current-reversal guards (Z3RBD, Z3XPU/Z3XD) exist because parallel-line faults reverse current mid-event; DCB's short delay (Z2PGS) waits for the block. Shorten them and you trade security for milliseconds.
  • An open remote breaker starves POTT. No elements assert at an open terminal → no permission. The 311C's echo logic covers it without 52b keying; know which your scheme relies on.
