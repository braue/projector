☰The rules, stated plainly

Line differential

  • 87L has no reach setting. The zone is defined by the CTs at each end, so it protects 100% of the line at high speed and needs no coordination with the neighbors' impedance.
  • Both ends must agree on time. A one-degree error in alignment at 60 Hz is 46 µs. Everything difficult about 87L follows from this.
  • Symmetrical channel: the relays synchronize on the channel itself. Asymmetrical channel (different delay each way, common on routed or Ethernet paths): you need an external time source at both ends instead.
  • The alpha plane is the characteristic. Plot the complex ratio of remote to local current: it sits at −1 (1∠180°) for load and external faults, and moves away for internal ones. Restraint is a region around −1, adjustable in both angular extent and radial reach.
  • External fault detection keeps CT saturation from tripping the line. Through current rising without differential current rising means the fault is outside — raise security immediately.
  • Long lines and cables need charging current compensation, or the standing capacitive current eats your sensitivity.
  • The channel is part of the protection. Monitor it, alarm it, and know exactly what the relay does when it fails.

In practice you get both. A modern line relay runs 87L as primary and carries full distance protection as backup for the channel-failed case. On the SEL-411L that means as many as five zones of phase and ground distance alongside the 87L function — the schemes themselves are covered in Distance Protection (21).

2The alpha plane

Rather than computing an operate and a restraint quantity, SEL's line differential plots the complex ratio k = Iremote / Ilocal. For load flow and for any fault outside the zone, current in equals current out, so the ratio is exactly −1 — magnitude 1 at 180°. Everything else moves away from that point in a characteristic direction.

The restraint region's angular extent and radial reach are both adjustable, which is how you trade sensitivity for tolerance of channel and CT imperfection. (Animated in the browser.)

Since 87LQ and 87LG have no inherent faulted-phase identification, single-pole tripping from those sensitive elements requires separate logic. The SEL-411L includes its own faulted-phase selection using symmetrical components of the phase differential currents; when differential is unavailable it falls back to single-ended identification from angular relationships in the local current alone.

3Making both ends agree on time

The relay must compare a local sample with the remote sample taken at the same instant. There are two ways to establish that, and choosing wrongly is the classic 87L design error.

Ask the network owner one question in writing: is the path symmetrical, and does it stay symmetrical after a protection switch? A route that reroutes on failure is asymmetrical by definition. (Animated in the browser.)

5Charging current compensation

A long line or a cable draws capacitive charging current that enters at the terminals and never leaves — from the differential element's point of view, it looks exactly like a standing internal fault. On a long EHV line or a substantial cable this can be a meaningful fraction of the pickup you wanted.

  • The method: compute the charging current from the measured terminal voltages and subtract it from the measured phase current. Because it is derived from the actual voltages rather than assumed, the compensation stays accurate for both faulted and unfaulted conditions.
  • It requires voltage. That is why loss of potential matters here: with no voltage, the compensation cannot be computed. The SEL-411L includes fallback logic for the LOP case, and the adaptive security feature raises 87L security when compensation is enabled but momentarily impossible.
  • When you need it: long overhead EHV lines, and essentially any significant length of cable. On short lines it is unnecessary.

6Multi-terminal, dual-breaker, and stub bus

  • Terminal count. The SEL-411L supports as many as three terminals over serial communications and as many as four over Ethernet, in three-pole or single-pole tripping modes.
  • Dual-breaker terminals. Each terminal can be a dual-breaker arrangement — ring bus or breaker-and-a-half — with individual breaker currents provided to the differential element rather than a pre-summed value. Keeping the breakers separate is what preserves security when one CT saturates.
  • Stub bus protection. When the line disconnect is open but both breakers remain closed, the short section between them is a “stub” that the line differential no longer covers. Disconnect status inputs and voltage elements enable a true restrained differential over that stub — high-speed, and with the security a restrained function gives in dual-breaker applications.
  • Platform compatibility is not assumed. The SEL-311L and SEL-411L use the same generalized alpha plane principle but are entirely separate hardware and firmware platforms and cannot be paired in one differential scheme. Both ends of an 87L scheme must be the same family.
Check platform and firmware compatibility before ordering. A line differential scheme is the one application where the far end's relay model is your problem. “They already have an SEL line relay at the other end” is not sufficient information.

The channel budget you actually care about. Latency (how long the data takes), symmetry (whether both directions match), stability (whether latency changes when the network reconfigures), and availability. Multiplexed networks are engineered for the first and last of those; you must ask specifically about the middle two. A network that reroutes around a failure without telling you is the worst case, because the protection was working perfectly right up until the reroute.

8What happens when the channel fails

  1. 87L is unavailable, immediately and completely. There is no degraded mode.
  2. Backup takes over. On a relay with distance protection this means falling back to time-stepped zones — slower at the far end, but selective. This is why full distance protection alongside 87L is standard practice rather than optional.
  3. The alarm must reach someone. A channel-failure alarm that goes only to a local LED is a protection outage nobody knows about. Map it, and treat it with the same urgency as a relay failure.
  4. Watch for intermittent channels. Repeated brief failures suggest a marginal optical budget, a failing multiplexer card, or a network that is reconverging. Trend the channel-quality measurement rather than only counting alarms.
  5. Know what a partial failure does. Degraded synchronization — not full loss — is the dangerous middle case, and it is exactly what the adaptive security feature is there for.

9Commissioning and gotchas

  • Prove CT ratios and polarity at both ends before the channel is even connected. A reversed CT at the remote end produces a differential equal to twice the through current — it will trip on load the moment the scheme is put in service.
  • Read the standing differential under load. This is the acceptance test. On a compensated line, check it both with and without charging-current compensation enabled.
  • Measure the channel round-trip delay and record it, then re-measure after any transport work. A changed latency is the first symptom of a rerouted path.
  • Test the asymmetry case if the channel is routed. Ask the transport owner to fail the primary path deliberately during commissioning and watch what the relay reports.
  • Confirm the synchronization method matches the channel. Channel-based sync on an asymmetrical path is the single most consequential settings error in 87L.
  • Verify the fallback. Pull the fiber, confirm the alarm, confirm distance backup is enabled and correctly set, then restore and confirm 87L returns.
  • Do not mix platforms. Both ends must be the same relay family — verify part numbers and firmware, not just the label on the panel.
  • Remember the stub. If the line disconnect can be opened with breakers closed, stub bus protection is not optional; without it, that section is unprotected.
