☰The rules, stated plainly

Coordination

  • Selectivity is the whole game: for any fault, the protective device nearest it clears, and every device upstream has margin to spare. Miscoordination means a whole feeder goes dark for one lateral's fault.
  • Traditional distribution coordination keeps a time-coordination interval of typically 0.2–0.5 s between devices at the fault current where their curves are closest — controlled by curve type, pickup, and time dial (SEL AG2023-23).
  • Check margin at the maximum fault current at the downstream device — inverse curves pinch together there, not at low current.
  • Fuse-saving: trip fast and reclose before the lateral fuse is damaged — fewer blown fuses, more momentary blinks for everyone. Fuse-blowing: wait for the fuse — fewer blinks, extended outage on the faulted lateral (AG2019-01).
  • Instantaneous elements and reclose sequences break curve-only coordination; that's what SH0 supervision and 79SEQ sequence coordination are for.

This page assumes the element basics — what 50/51 are, the U1–U5/C1–C5 curve equations, pickup variants, torque control. That's the Protection Elements Primer in atlas; this page is about making several of those devices work as a stack.

1The coordination problem: a radial feeder

A distribution feeder is a chain of protective devices in series, each one backing up everything below it. A typical trunk runs from the substation with fused laterals hung off it and often a line recloser partway out (AG2019-01: "it is common practice to minimize exposure of the trunk to a fault by fusing each lateral").

Bus / main breakersubstation source-side protection — last resort, slowest
←

Every device between the source and the fault carries the same fault current. The only thing that decides who clears it is time — so coordination is arranging the devices' time-current characteristics (TCCs) so that, at every credible fault current, the clearing order matches the geographic order. Two complications make it interesting: fuses can't reclose or change their minds, and reclosers deliberately trip faster than the fuses below them on early shots, then slower on later ones.

2Reading a TCC plot

A TCC plot is log-log: current across, operating time down the page as current rises. Everything about coordination is visible on it at a glance:

  • Relay curves are drawn in multiples of pickup (M). The pickup setting slides a curve left/right on the amps axis; the time dial slides it up/down without changing its shape (TD 0.50–15.00 for U-curves on both the 751 and 751A; C-curve TD is 0.05–1.00 on the 751A but 0.01–1.50 on the 751; 0.01 steps. The 751 also adds IEEE E1–E3 curves on the U.S. dial range). A whole time-dial family nests — parallel curves on log axes.
  • Curve family sets the slope. Moderately inverse (U1/C1) is flat; extremely inverse (U4/C3) collapses as ~1/(M²−1) — an I²t character. Pick the family whose steepness matches the device you must coordinate with.
  • Devices below you must plot below-and-left of you across the whole overlap of currents both can see, with the margin band (§3) between the curves.
  • Curves are bands, not lines. The SEL-751/751A published tolerance is ±1.5 cycles plus ±4% between 2 and 30 multiples of pickup; drawn margins must survive that plus breaker interrupting time.

Two nested TCCs (both SEL U3 very inverse, computed from the SEL curve equations — the 751 and 751A publish identical U/C-curve math) and a fault-current line. The rising dot is elapsed time on the log axis: the recloser's curve is crossed first, the fault is cleared, and the feeder relay's timer stops with the amber margin band unspent. Margin is checked here, at maximum fault current, because that's where inverse curves crowd together. ▶ Animated in the HTML edition.

3The coordination time interval

The margin between adjacent curves is not generosity — it's a budget that real hardware spends. SEL AG2023-23 gives the working number for distribution: "a time-coordination interval of typically 0.2–0.5 seconds between each recloser control," set by manipulating curve type, pickup, and time dial. What the interval has to absorb:

ConsumerWhy it eats margin
Downstream clearing timeThe upstream curve must clear the downstream device's total clearing time (breaker/interrupter operate time included), not just its curve. AG2019-01's published recloser curves explicitly "include the mechanical operating time of the recloser and the element assertion time."
Curve toleranceSEL-751/751A curve timing accuracy: ±1.5 cycles plus ±4% between 2 and 30 multiples of pickup. Both devices wobble; margins must cover both wobbles.
Measurement differencesAG2023-23 lists why nominally identical devices seeing identical fault current may not operate together: "CT error, relay error, section load, and processing interval differences."
Disk state (EM relays)An electromechanical relay upstream may carry leftover disk travel from a previous shot — see §6.
Where to check: compute margin at the maximum fault current available at the downstream device. Inverse curves converge as current rises, so if the margin holds there it holds everywhere the downstream device can see. Then confirm the upstream pickup still sees (with sensitivity margin) the minimum fault at the far end of the zone it must back up.

4Fuse curves on the plot

A fuse brings two curves to the TCC, and coordination happens between them (general fuse practice; SEL guides refer to the same physics as fuse "damage"):

Minimum melting

The fastest the element can start to melt. To save a fuse, the recloser's fast trip — including its mechanical operate time — must complete below this curve, with margin, at every fault current the lateral can deliver. Partial melt is real damage: AG2019-01 warns a late fast trip "may attempt to save an already damaged fuse."

Total clearing

Melt plus arcing — when the circuit is actually broken. To let a fuse blow selectively, the upstream delay curve must sit above this curve with margin: AG2019-01's delay curve "is chosen to coordinate with both fuse types, allowing adequate time for either fuse to blow before operating."

  • Saveability is a fault-current question. In AG2019-01's worked feeder, the recloser fast curve can save 100T fuses only below 3000 A and 65T fuses only below 2000 A of available fault current — above that, the fuse melts faster than the fast trip can clear, and those laterals default to fuse-blowing.
  • Why extremely inverse mates with fuses: fuse melting is thermal, an I²t phenomenon, so fuse curves are very steep. The U4/C3 extremely inverse equations are t ∝ 1/(M²−1) — the same I²t character — so an extremely inverse relay curve runs roughly parallel to a fuse curve instead of crossing it. A flat (moderately inverse) curve above a fuse at low current will cut under it at high current — a hidden miscoordination.
  • Fuses don't reset. Every fault-current excursion, including ones a recloser interrupts, deposits permanent melt progress. Repeated shots against a marginal fuse walk it toward nuisance failure.

5Fuse-saving vs fuse-blowing

Both schemes are legitimate; they trade different outages (definitions per AG2019-01):

Fuse-saving (trip-saving)Fuse-blowing
MechanismRecloser/relay trips on a fast curve below the fuse minimum melt, then recloses. Temporary fault gone → fuse intact, service restored.Recloser operation is delayed so the lateral fuse clears first; only the faulted lateral drops.
Temporary faultMomentary blink for the whole feeder; nothing to replace.Fuse blows anyway — extended outage for that lateral until a crew replaces it.
Permanent faultFast shots exhaust, scheme falls back to delayed trip / fuse blows on a later shot.Fuse blows; rest of feeder never sees an interruption.
CostMore momentary outages for the entire feeder — power-quality complaints.More truck rolls and long outages on laterals with temporary faults.

The classic fuse-saving implementation in an SEL feeder relay is one line of trip logic (SEL-351 manual, its own fuse-saving example): TR = 51PT + 51GT + 50P1 * SH0. The instantaneous 50P1 — set fast and sensitive enough to beat the fuse — is allowed to trip only at shot = 0 (SH0 asserted). After the first trip-and-reclose the shot counter advances, SH0 drops out, and 50P1 is locked out of the trip equation: the reclose lands with only the delayed 51 curves active, giving the fuse time to clear a persistent fault.

One permanent fault on a fused lateral under TR = 51PT + 51GT + 50P1·SH0. First shot: the instantaneous element wins the race against the fuse's minimum-melt curve. On reclose, SH0 = 0 has disarmed the fast trip, so the fuse and the slow 51 curve race instead — the fuse, closer to the fault and already part-melted, clears first. The feeder relay's breaker stays closed; only the lateral is lost. ▶ Animated in the HTML edition.

Choosing per-lateral, not per-feeder: AG2019-01's whole point is that the choice doesn't have to be global. With SEL-FT50 line-mounted fault transmitters reporting through an SEL-FR12 into MIRRORED BITS, a recloser control (SEL-651RA) can default to fuse-blowing and enable the fast curve only when the fault indication comes from a saveable lateral — TR3X = 51P1T AND (SV11 OR …) AND NOT SV07T — or default to fuse-saving and block it for unsaveable/underground laterals. Physics still rules the edges: the fault indication takes ~1–2 cycles to arrive, so "fast curves must be set to operate slower than two cycles" to be blockable, and SV07T blocks saving once the fast curve has already timed out against a now-damaged fuse. Underground laterals get reclosing blocked outright, since their faults are rarely temporary.

6Electromechanical reset — coordinating with spinning disks

An EM induction-disk relay times by disk travel, and when current drops below pickup the disk doesn't snap home — it spins back slowly. A numerical relay's 51 element, by contrast, resets in 1 cycle by default. Each SEL-751/751A 51 element carries an EM RESET DELAY setting (51_RS := Y/N): set Y and the element resets on the published electromechanical induction-disk emulation reset-time equation instead — e.g. U3: tr = TD · 3.88/(1−M²) for M < 1, with each curve family having its own tr.

Why it matters for coordination: reclose sequences and downstream fuse/recloser operations subject every relay in the chain to repeated bursts of fault current with short gaps. If the upstream device is electromechanical, its disk accumulates travel across those bursts. A digital relay below it that resets fully between bursts restarts its timing from zero each shot while the EM relay above creeps toward trip — the margin you drew on the TCC quietly evaporates. Enabling EM reset emulation makes both devices "remember" the same way, so the coordination drawn for a single shot survives a multi-shot sequence. (Corollary from §10 of the Primer: a 51 that seems to time "too fast" under test may just be part-way travelled from a previous injection with 51_RS := Y.)

7Sequence coordination — 79SEQ keeps the relay in step

A feeder relay coordinated with a downstream line recloser has a timing problem the TCC can't show: the recloser runs a sequence (typically fast curve twice, then slow curve twice), so what the relay must coordinate with changes shot by shot. The SEL-751/751A answer is the 79SEQ SELOGIC setting (identical in both manuals):

  • Detection: if the relay is not tripping, the breaker is closed, and an overcurrent pickup named in 79SEQ asserts for ≥1.25 cycles and then drops out, a downstream device just cleared a fault. The relay increments its own shot counter anyway (up to shot = 4, even past last shot) and reloads the reset timer with 79RSD.
  • Effect: trip logic supervised by shot bits steps down in aggressiveness in lockstep with the recloser. Manual example — 51P1T coordinated with the recloser fast curve, 51A/B/C coordinated with its slow curve: TR := 51P1T AND (SH0 OR SH1) OR 51AT OR 51BT OR 51CT. After two recloser fast operations the shot counter sits at 2, SH0/SH1 are gone, and the faster-set 51P1T can no longer overreach for faults beyond the recloser now clearing on its slow curve.
  • Refinement: 79SEQ := 79RS AND 51P1P AND SH0 stops the counter at shot = 1, so a long recloser sequence downstream doesn't ratchet the relay to shot = 4 — where a coincident fault in the relay's own zone would trip it straight to Lockout instead of leaving it a reclose.
  • Timer discipline: 79RSD (default 15 s) must outlast the recloser's whole sequence, or the shot counter resets mid-sequence and the fast element re-arms at exactly the wrong moment. Sequence coordination does not change the reclosing state — a relay in Reset stays in Reset. Factory default: 79SEQ := 0 (disabled).
The instantaneous-element trap: a 50 element has no curve to coordinate on — set to reach past a downstream recloser or fuse, it will simply beat them every time and de-select the whole feeder. The two containment tools are both above: supervise it with shot bits (50P1 * SH0, §5) so it only fires on the first shot, and let 79SEQ step it down when the downstream device is mid-sequence. High-set 50s that reach only to the next device's bus are the exception — they can't see past it, so they can't miscoordinate with it.

8When curves run out: high-density coordination

Every device pair in a chain costs one CTI (0.2–0.5 s). Stack enough reclosers in series and either the substation clearing time grows absurd or the margins compress into miscoordination — AG2023-23: "traditional time coordination using time-overcurrent curves becomes increasingly difficult to impossible." The HDC voltage-guided reclosing scheme (SEL-651R/-651RA) abandons per-device time grading inside a group:

  • Same settings everywhere: all reclosers in a coordination group share one delay curve, pickup, and time dial — so for an in-group fault, several of them trip together. That's deliberate, not miscoordination; only the groups are time-coordinated with each other.
  • Voltage restores order: after the trip, only the recloser with healthy source-side voltage recloses (dead-line threshold 20% of nominal, 27YPP1P/27ZPP1P). Each device below it sees voltage return, waits ~3 s of confirmed-healthy stall (79STL3P + SV dropout 180 cycles), then recloses — restoration marches down the line one segment at a time.
  • Fast-on-close: at each close the control arms a fast curve for 3 s (51PSW switched by SVzzT), then reverts to the delay curve. The closing device is thus always on fast while everything above it is back on delay — coordination re-created dynamically with identical settings. Second-harmonic blocking (EHBL2 = Y, HBL2P = 10, per-phase bits) keeps the fast curve from tripping on energization inrush.
  • Endgame: the device just upstream of the faulted segment closes into the fault, clears on fast, runs its shot sequence, and locks out — selectivity achieved without a single drawn margin. A 5-minute no-voltage timer (SVyyPU = 18000 cycles) drives stragglers to lockout for crew safety.

9Practical gotchas

  • Cold-load pickup breaks the load/fault boundary. After an extended outage, diversity is lost — reclosing picks up every stalled compressor at once, and inrush can momentarily assert time-overcurrent pickups. The 751/751A manuals' own example: closing from Lockout, "there could be cold load inrush current that momentarily picks up a time-overcurrent element" — harmless to reset timing there, but the same current will trip a marginal fast curve. Mitigations in the sources: harmonic blocking on fast curves (AG2023-23), 79BRS := (51P1P OR 51G1P) AND 79CY to stop trip-reclose cycling, the SEL-751's dedicated cold-load pickup element (ECLPU: outage-duration logic asserts CLPU1/CLPU2 bits to torque-control overcurrent elements to raised trip levels while inrush decays — the 751A has no equivalent), and — for schemes that assume identical pickups (HDC) — AG2023-23's warning that activating a cold-load scheme on one device means manually activating it in all upstream devices, or the shared-pickup assumption silently dies.
  • Settings groups are coordination in disguise. The SEL-751A holds three full settings groups (SS1–SS3), the SEL-751 four (SS1–SS4, active-group bits SG1–SG4) — selected by SELOGIC (priority over the GROUP n command) with qualifying delay TGR (default 3 s). An alternate-source or backfeed configuration usually needs a different curve set — that's what the groups are for. But mind the transition: the relay is disabled for <1 s during a group change, elements and timers reset, and the reclosing shot counter is driven to last shot — a relay mid-open-interval goes straight to Lockout. Group changes during a live sequence are not free.
  • Negative-sequence elements coordinate as "equivalent phase" elements. SEL-351 appendix method for a radial system: derive the settings as if coordinating a phase element with the worst downstream phase device, then multiply the pickup by 3 (elements operate on 3I2); time dial and curve carry over unchanged, and load current can be ignored — the sensitivity win of 51Q without a separate coordination study.
  • A blown fuse you can't see from current: a blown high-side fuse on a delta-wye transformer produces no fault current at all — just a voltage signature: one low-side phase-to-phase voltage collapses to zero while the other two drop to 87% of nominal (a blown relay-VT fuse instead gives 58%). AG2011-19's SEL-651R detector: any 27YPP below 40% AND any 59YPP above 70%, through a 5 s SV timer (SV42PU := 300 cycles) into the trip equation — tripping the feeder beats cooking three-phase motors on negative-sequence current. Same idea on the SEL-351 in AG97-11.
  • Margins are spent by things not on the plot: recloser mechanical time, ±1.5 cyc ± 4% curve tolerance, CT error, and processing-interval differences (§3). If the coordination study's margin is exactly 0.2 s on paper, it's negative in the field.
