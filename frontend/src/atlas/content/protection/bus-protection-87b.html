☰The rules, stated plainly

Bus differential

  • A bus fault is the worst fault in the station. Every source feeds it, nothing limits it, and clearing it means opening every breaker on the bus. So it must be fast — the SEL-487B operates in under one cycle — and it must never operate for anything else.
  • The principle is Kirchhoff. Sum every current entering the bus. Zero means healthy; non-zero means a fault inside the zone.
  • CT saturation is the whole engineering problem. A heavy external fault saturates the CT carrying the outflow, its output collapses, and the sum stops being zero. Everything else in a modern bus relay exists to survive that.
  • The zone must follow the switching. On a bus with disconnects, which terminal belongs to which zone changes when a switch moves. The relay reads 89a/89b contacts and reassigns currents dynamically.
  • A check zone is a second opinion. An independent differential across all sources and feeders, unaffected by zone selection, supervising the zone elements.
  • Faults between the breaker and the CT are outside the bus zone. They need dedicated end-zone logic and usually a transfer trip.

1The principle, and the problem

Sum the currents at every terminal of the bus. In a healthy bus, or for a fault anywhere outside the zone, the sum is zero: everything that comes in goes out. For a fault on the bus, the sum equals the fault current. That is the whole idea, and it gives bus differential its two great virtues — absolute selectivity and no need for coordination delay.

The difficulty is that a bus differential zone can have a dozen or more terminals, with wildly different current magnitudes, and during an external fault one CT carries the entire fault current while the others share it. That one CT is the one most likely to saturate.

The security-versus-speed trade is resolved in time, not in setting. Rather than desensitizing the differential permanently, the relay makes an early judgment about where the fault is and adapts. This is why the external-fault detection window matters so much: it must complete before saturation begins, which is why the specification is quoted in milliseconds rather than cycles.

3Dynamic zone selection

On a bus with sectionalizing disconnects, the boundary of a differential zone is not fixed — it depends on which switches are closed. Older schemes handled this with banks of auxiliary relays switching CT circuits, an arrangement that was both a maintenance burden and a source of open-CT hazards.

The SEL-487B takes the disconnect auxiliary contacts directly as digital inputs and reassigns currents to differential elements in software. SELogic control equations and zone selection logic assign each terminal to the correct zone, and the arrangement is inspectable at any time with the ZONE command, which prints the terminals and bus zones currently assigned to each protection zone.

The auxiliary contact timing matters: during the open-to-close transition the 89b must open before main-contact arcing starts, and 89a must close within that transition window. (Animated in the browser.)

4End-zone (dead-zone) faults

The CT defines the zone boundary, but the breaker is what interrupts. A fault in the short stretch between them belongs to whichever zone contains it — and opening that zone's breaker does not clear it, because the source is on the other side.

  • The mechanism: a fault on the line side of the CT but the bus side of the breaker is outside the bus differential zone. The line protection sees it and opens the remote end, but the local breaker opening does not remove the bus's contribution.
  • The remedy: use the breaker's 52a contact in a SELogic control equation so that when the breaker is open, that terminal's current is removed from the differential calculation. The bus differential then sees the fault as internal and clears the bus, while a transfer trip to the remote end clears the other source.
  • The cost: for this class of fault, the bus is tripped. That is accepted — there is no way to clear it otherwise.
This is why breaker-failure and bus differential usually live in the same relay. Both need the same currents, the same breaker status, and the same transfer-trip outputs. The SEL-487B provides breaker-failure protection for all supported breakers, including retrip logic and transfer-trip initiation — see Breaker Failure.

6Beyond differential: what else a bus relay carries

  • Backup overcurrent per terminal. Instantaneous and inverse-time elements provide backup for each terminal.
  • Breaker failure for every breaker on the bus, with retrip and transfer-trip initiation built in.
  • Six independent settings groups, selectable by control input, command, or programmable condition — the practical mechanism for maintenance modes, seasonal configurations, and contingency settings.
  • Automation capacity: local and remote control bits, protection and automation latches, and up to 1000 lines of automation logic — enough to build the bus's interlocking scheme inside the protection relay rather than beside it.
  • Battery monitoring with an alarm contact that catches a battery that is only weak during trip or close operations — a genuinely useful diagnostic for the failure mode described in Station DC Systems.
  • Digital secondary systems. The SEL-487B-2 can be ordered as an IEC 61850 Sampled Values subscriber (up to 24 SV merging units with the five-port Ethernet card) or as a TiDL relay receiving current and voltage from up to eight SEL-TMU merging units over point-to-point fiber. The TiDL variant synchronizes data collection itself, removing the dependence on an external clock for protection.
  • Selective protection disabling. On digital-secondary variants, losing communications with a merging unit means losing analog data; the relay selectively disables the affected protection functions using hard-coded logic or torque-control equations, rather than misoperating on missing data.

7Commissioning notes

  1. Prove every CT's ratio and polarity individually before energizing. On a twelve-terminal bus, one reversed CT produces a standing differential equal to twice that terminal's load current.
  2. Verify the zone configuration with the ZONE command in each credible switching state, not just the normal one. Operate the disconnects and watch the assignment change.
  3. Check the 89a/89b contact timing against the transition-zone requirement. A disconnect whose auxiliary contacts change late leaves the relay with a wrong zone map during the switching operation.
  4. Test the check zone independently. It should assert for a simulated internal fault and stay deasserted for a simulated external one.
  5. Exercise CT supervision by opening a shorted CT circuit on the test set and confirming the relay alarms rather than trips.
  6. Measure the standing differential under load once the bus is energized. It should be a small fraction of a percent. Anything larger is a wiring or ratio error, and it will not get better under fault conditions.

8Gotchas

  • A terminal left out of the zone configuration produces a differential equal to that terminal's current. It will read as a slowly growing problem as load rises, and it can trip the bus on a cold morning.
  • Disconnect auxiliary contacts that lie. Misadjusted or corroded 89a/89b contacts are common on older switches, and the relay's zone map is only as good as they are. Alarm on the disagreeing combination.
  • Auxiliary CTs added “to match ratios” when the relay does not need them. They add burden, saturate earlier than the main CTs, and undermine the very security you were trying to build.
  • Bus differential disabled for maintenance and not re-enabled. Use a settings group with a clear indication and a SCADA point, never an out-of-service jumper someone has to remember.
  • Trip outputs going to the wrong lockout. A bus trip must open every breaker in the zone; verify each one, not a sample.
  • Forgetting that the check zone has its own CT set assumptions. If a terminal is excluded from the check zone by mistake, the supervision that was supposed to be independent will simply agree with the error.
