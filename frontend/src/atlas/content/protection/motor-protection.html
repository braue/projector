☰The rules, stated plainly

Motors

  • The enemy is heat, not current. A motor is destroyed by accumulated thermal damage to insulation, so the primary element is a thermal model (49) that integrates I²t, not a simple overcurrent pickup.
  • Starting looks exactly like a fault — typically 6–8 times full-load current for several seconds. Every element has to be secure through that, which is why a motor relay needs to know it is starting.
  • Negative-sequence current heats the rotor disproportionately. A small voltage unbalance produces a much larger current unbalance, and the resulting double-frequency rotor currents heat far faster than positive-sequence current of the same magnitude. The thermal model must include it.
  • Single-phasing is the classic motor killer. The motor keeps turning, the remaining phases overload, and nothing looks dramatic until the windings fail.
  • Thermal capacity is a budget you spend and slowly get back. Two successive cold starts may be allowed; a third is often not, and the relay's starts-per-hour and time-between-starts logic exists to enforce that.
  • Set the relay from the motor's data, not from the feeder's. Full-load amps, locked-rotor current and time, service factor, and the thermal limit curve are motor nameplate and manufacturer data.

1The life of a motor, in current

Everything a motor relay does is anchored to this one waveform: a very large inrush and starting current for a few seconds, then a long steady run near full-load amps, punctuated by whatever the load does to it.

Distinguishing these three regions is most of what a motor relay does; the elements are just the consequences. (Animated in the browser.)

2The thermal model (49)

Rather than a fixed time-current curve, a motor relay maintains a running estimate of how hot the machine is, expressed as percent thermal capacity used (%TCU). Current heats; time cools. The relay trips when %TCU reaches 100.

The SEL-710 and SEL-849 both implement a patented thermal model that tracks stator and rotor heating separately, includes negative-sequence heating, and behaves differently while accelerating than while running. On the SEL-849 the relay trips when either the stator or the rotor thermal estimate reaches 100 percent.

A motor thermal element has memory. Restoring a tripped motor by cycling power does not reset the machine's temperature, and a relay that forgets is worse than one that remembers. (Animated in the browser.)

Three ways to set it

The SEL-849 data sheet describes three routes to the same model, and the same choice exists on the SEL-710 family:

MethodWhat you enterUse when
IEC or NEMA trip classClass 5, 10, 20, or 30You have little motor data and need conventional, defensible protection. The class number is roughly the allowed locked-rotor time in seconds at 6× FLA
Motor nameplate ratingsFull-load current, locked-rotor current, hot stall (safe stall) time, overload pickup levelThe normal case. Gives a model matched to the actual machine
Standard thermal limit curveOne of 45 built-in curvesYou are replacing an existing relay and must emulate its behavior, or the motor manufacturer specifies a curve
Get the hot and cold stall times right. “Safe stall time” from the motor manufacturer is given for a cold motor and a hot motor, and they differ substantially. Using the cold value for a machine that starts frequently sets the protection above what the motor can actually survive.

3Unbalance, single-phasing, and phase reversal

  • 46 — current unbalance. Trips for single-phasing or heavy unbalance. This is in addition to the unbalance contribution already inside the thermal model, and it exists because a severe unbalance needs a faster response than thermal accumulation provides.
  • 47 — phase reversal. Detects incorrect rotation and trips after a delay. On the SEL-849 this works even without voltage inputs, from current alone — genuinely useful after any maintenance that disturbed the cabling.
  • Why unbalance is so damaging: the motor's negative-sequence impedance is close to its locked-rotor impedance, so a few percent of negative-sequence voltage drives a much larger percentage of negative-sequence current, and that current induces double-frequency currents in the rotor, where the heating is concentrated and least well cooled.
A blown fuse upstream is the classic case. The motor continues to run on two phases, drawing heavily unbalanced current, sounding rough, and heating the rotor fast. Nothing about the total current is dramatic. The 46 element and the thermal model's negative-sequence term are what save the machine.

Voltage-based additions

Both relays offer optional voltage inputs, which unlock over/undervoltage, over/underfrequency, directional power, power factor, and loss of potential. On the SEL-849 the voltage inputs connect directly up to 690 V AC and can be configured as a single phase-to-phase, a single phase-to-neutral, open-delta, or four-wire wye. Undervoltage matters operationally: a voltage dip that stalls a motor is followed by an inrush from every motor on the bus when voltage returns, which is why undervoltage automatic restart logic is a standard scheme rather than an exotic one.

Synchronous machines

The SEL-710-5 offers an optional synchronous motor card that adds starting control, power-factor or reactive-power closed-loop regulation, and loss-of-field, out-of-step, pull-out, field resistance, field voltage, and field current protection. A synchronous motor that loses field pulls out of step and draws heavy reactive current — it must be detected and dealt with quickly.

5Monitoring and what to send to SCADA

Motor relays are unusually rich in operational data, and much of it is more valuable than the trip contacts.

%TCU (stator and rotor) How close the machine is to a thermal trip, right now. The single best predictor of an impending motor outage.
Motor start report Both relays capture a dedicated start report — current, voltage, duration, and thermal usage for each start. The SEL-710-5 adds exciter voltage and current for synchronous machines.
Start trending and statistics Starting current and time drifting upward across months is a mechanical problem announcing itself early.
RTD temperatures Winding and bearing temperatures as analogs, not just alarm contacts.
Starts remaining / time until start permitted Answers the operator's actual question, and prevents the “why won't it start” call.
Load profile and demand Both relays record load profile; useful for process troubleshooting well beyond protection.

Both relays speak DNP3 (serial and LAN/WAN), Modbus RTU and TCP, IEC 61850, EtherNet/IP, and SEL protocols, with an integrated web server for events, SER, and start reports. Integration practice is in SCADA Integration.

6Gotchas

  • Setting FLA from the starter's rating instead of the motor's. The relay's entire thermal model scales from FLA; get it wrong and every element is wrong by the same factor.
  • Ignoring the service factor. A 1.15 service factor motor is allowed to run continuously above nameplate FLA. Setting the overload pickup at exactly 1.00 produces nuisance trips on a machine that is operating within its rating.
  • Instantaneous phase pickup set below locked-rotor current. The motor will trip on every start, and someone will “fix” it by disabling the element.
  • Forgetting that thermal state persists. A relay that has just tripped thermally will block the restart, and that block is protection working. Do not defeat it — wait, or investigate why the machine is hot.
  • Residual ground on a high-resistance grounded system without a CBCT. The available ground current is a few amperes; a residual element derived from phase CTs cannot resolve it. Specify the core-balance CT at design time, not after commissioning fails.
  • VFD-fed motors are a different problem. Both relays support VFD configuration explicitly, but the current waveform, frequency, and the drive's own protection all change the picture. Do not apply an across-the-line configuration to a drive-fed machine.
  • RTD wiring errors read as plausible temperatures. A swapped or shorted RTD pair produces a believable number. Verify each channel against an actual measurement during commissioning.
  • Contactors are not breakers. A contactor cannot interrupt fault current; for short circuits the relay must trip an upstream device, and the coordination scheme must reflect that.
