☰The rules, stated plainly

Closing a breaker between two live sources

  • Three things must match: voltage magnitude, frequency (so the angle is not running away), and phase angle at the instant the contacts touch.
  • Close out of phase and the difference appears across the machine or the line. On a generator that can mean shaft torque exceeding a three-phase fault; on a system tie it means a large transient power swing.
  • The relay closes early on purpose. The breaker takes tens of milliseconds to close, so the command is issued at an advance angle chosen so the contacts touch at zero degrees.
  • Dead-line and dead-bus permissives are separate decisions, and enabling the wrong combination is the most common way a sync-check scheme quietly fails.
  • A transformer between the two voltage sources introduces a fixed angle that must be compensated in the relay, or the element will never permit at true zero.
  • Test what it blocks, not just what it permits. A scheme wired backwards permits everything, and normal operation never reveals it.

1The three quantities

All three conditions must hold simultaneously. A scheme with a generous angle window and no slip limit will happily close a machine that is sweeping through zero at speed. (Animated in the browser.)

2Live, dead, and the permissive matrix

A sync-check element does more than compare two live sources: it also decides whether closing is allowed when one or both sides are dead. Each combination is a separate enable, and the right set depends entirely on the application.

Running (bus)Incoming (line)NameTypical use

Enabling all four is the classic mistake. It looks harmless during commissioning — everything closes — and it removes the entire protection. If both ends of a line can close on live-bus/dead-line, an operator can energize the line from both ends independently, and the second close is out of synchronism with no supervision at all. Decide which end is the source, document it, and set the two relays differently.

3The angle a transformer adds

When the running and incoming voltages are measured on opposite sides of a delta–wye transformer, they differ by a fixed 30° (or a multiple, depending on the vector group) even when the system is perfectly in synchronism. A sync-check element set to permit at 0° will then never permit — or, if the window is wide enough, will permit at a genuine 30° error.

  • The fix is a compensation setting: tell the relay the fixed angle between its two voltage inputs so it compares like with like.
  • PT connection also matters. One side wye-connected and the other open-delta introduces its own 30° relationship, independent of any transformer.
  • Verify by measurement, not by calculation. With the breaker closed and the system solidly connected, the relay should show an angle difference of essentially zero after compensation. If it does not, the compensation is wrong — and you have just found it under the safest possible conditions.

The vector-group arithmetic behind this is in Power Transformers; the wye versus open-delta PT distinction is in CT & PT Fundamentals.

Open transition versus closed transition is a policy decision, not a technical detail. Closed transition (make before break) means no customer interruption but requires synchronism and briefly parallels two sources — raising fault duty for that instant. Open transition (break before make) is simple and always safe but drops load. Know which one your scheme implements before you write the logic, because the two need entirely different supervision.

6Commissioning

  1. Confirm which voltage input is which. Running versus incoming is a settings and wiring fact. Swapping them can invert the compensation and the dead-side logic.
  2. Verify angle compensation with the breaker closed. With both sides solidly connected the compensated angle difference should read essentially zero. This is the cheapest, safest check available.
  3. Prove each permissive individually. Live/live, live/dead, dead/live, dead/dead — apply each condition with a test set and confirm the element permits or blocks exactly as designed.
  4. Prove the blocks. Apply an angle outside the window, a voltage difference outside the window, and a slip above the limit, and confirm the close is blocked in each case. This is the test that finds a reversed scheme.
  5. Measure the breaker's actual closing time and confirm the advance angle setting derives from it.
  6. Check the interaction with reclosing. Does the 79 wait for sync check, and what does it do when sync check never permits? A reclose that stalls indefinitely waiting for permission is a design decision that should be explicit.

7Gotchas

  • All four permissives enabled. The scheme becomes decorative. Decide the source end and set the two ends differently.
  • Angle compensation omitted across a transformer. Either it never permits (and someone widens the window until it does — now permitting a genuine 30° error), or it permits at 30°.
  • Dead-voltage threshold set too high. A line with capacitive coupling from a parallel circuit can show appreciable voltage while genuinely de-energized. Set the dead threshold low enough that induced voltage does not read as “live,” and high enough that real voltage does.
  • Slip limit left at a default. A generous default that made bench testing easy will permit closes the machine cannot survive.
  • Advance angle from the nameplate. Breaker mechanisms slow with age and cold. Measure.
  • Sync-check voltage lost and nobody noticed. A blown VT fuse on the incoming side makes that side look dead, which may enable a live-bus/dead-line close into an energized line. Loss-of-potential supervision on sync-check inputs is not optional.
  • Assuming two feeders from the same station are in step. They usually are. “Usually” is not a protection basis when the alternative is a large out-of-step close.
