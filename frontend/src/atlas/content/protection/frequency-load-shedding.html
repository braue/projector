☰The rules, stated plainly

Frequency

  • Frequency is the balance indicator. Mechanical power in equals electrical load plus losses, or the rotating masses make up the difference and speed changes. Falling frequency means not enough generation, right now.
  • Load shedding buys time. Deliberately dropping some load restores the balance before the whole system collapses. Selective, temporary, and staged.
  • Stage it. First stage just below normal, later stages for progressively larger excursions, each with its own delay and its own block of load.
  • Frequency measurement needs voltage. Every frequency element must be blocked below a voltage threshold, or it will measure nonsense during a fault and shed load for no reason.
  • Rate of change (ROCOF) is faster but less secure. It reacts to the initial slope, which is proportional to the size of the imbalance — useful for severe events, hazardous for stable oscillations.
  • Restoration is the hard part. Shedding is automatic and takes seconds; restoring is manual, coordinated, and takes far longer than anyone expects.

1Why frequency falls

In a stable system, total mechanical power input from the generators equals the sum of all connected load plus system losses. Disturb that balance and the rotors of the turbine-generators — which act as repositories of kinetic energy — make up the difference. Insufficient mechanical input slows them down, and frequency declines in proportion.

The rate of decline depends on two things: how large the imbalance is, and how much rotating inertia the system has. A large generation loss on a small island collapses frequency in seconds; the same loss on a large interconnected system produces a shallow dip that governors correct.

Shedding is not a nicety. Without it, generator underfrequency protection takes the rest of the system down to protect the individual machines. (Animated in the browser.)

2Designing a shedding scheme

SEL's application guidance for underfrequency load shedding lists five things to settle before any settings are entered:

Number of stagesThree to five is typical. More stages means finer granularity and more coordination work.
Frequency setting per stageStage 1 just below normal system frequency; later stages for progressively larger excursions.
Time delay per stageThe initial shed delay and the delays between subsequent sheds. Long enough to ride through transients, short enough to matter.
MVA shed at each stageThe load actually dropped. This is a system-planning number, allocated down to individual feeders.
Maximum overloadThe worst imbalance the scheme must survive. Sets how much total load has to be sheddable.
And, in practice: who gets shedFeeder selection is a policy matter — hospitals, water, and critical infrastructure are typically excluded, which means somebody else is shed twice as often.

How the elements are built

On SEL relays a frequency element is a level detector on measured system frequency plus a timer. A single element type serves both directions: with a pickup below nominal it acts as an underfrequency detector; with a pickup above nominal it acts as an overfrequency detector. Each element has a corresponding timer for the stage delay. Relays typically provide several such elements — the SEL-387E used in SEL's load-shedding application guide provides six — so one relay can implement a complete multi-stage scheme.

Use the instantaneous frequency elements for testing only. SEL's guidance is explicit on this: the undelayed outputs exist so you can verify pickup on a test set. A production scheme sheds through the timed outputs, because a momentary frequency excursion during a fault is not a generation deficit.

3Undervoltage blocking — the setting nobody should skip

Frequency is measured from a voltage waveform. During a fault, or when the PT circuit fails, that waveform may be depressed, distorted, or absent — and the frequency the relay computes from it becomes meaningless.

  • Every frequency element is supervised by an undervoltage block. On the SEL-387E this is element 27B81P, which disables the frequency elements when system voltage falls below its setting; equivalent blocks exist across the SEL relay families.
  • Set it above the voltage at which frequency measurement stops being trustworthy and below normal operating voltage with margin. Too high and a legitimate voltage sag blocks shedding when you need it; too low and a fault produces spurious sheds.
  • The measurement source matters. The SEL-387E measures system frequency from A-phase voltage. A single-phase measurement means a fault or fuse failure on that one phase disables the whole scheme — another reason the undervoltage block, and an alarm on it, is essential.
  • Alarm the block. A load-shedding scheme that has silently been blocked for months by a blown PT fuse is worse than no scheme, because everyone believes it is there.

4Rate of change of frequency

The initial slope of the frequency decline is proportional to the size of the imbalance divided by the system inertia. Measuring that slope (81R, or ROCOF) therefore tells you how bad the event is well before the frequency has actually fallen very far.

Reacts toFrequency crossing a thresholdHow fast frequency is changing
SpeedWaits for the decline to developCan act within a few cycles of a large event
SecurityGood — frequency is a slow, system-wide quantityLower — power swings, faults, and local oscillations all produce apparent rate

SEL's application guidance shows the standard combination: an underfrequency element for the level, with a second element and a latch arranged so that a fast rate of decline escalates the response — shedding a deeper stage immediately rather than waiting for frequency to reach it. On DER interties, ROCOF and vector shift serve a different purpose entirely — detecting that the local generator has been islanded. See DER Interconnection.

Inertia is falling, and it changes these settings. As synchronous generation is displaced by inverter-based resources, system inertia drops and the same MW imbalance produces a steeper frequency slope. Schemes designed decades ago against a high-inertia system may act too late. This is an active area of change — check the current regional requirement rather than copying an old settings file.

This is why level detection stays the backbone and rate detection is an accelerator, not a replacement. (Animated in the browser.)

5Overfrequency, and the other direction

  • Overfrequency (81O) means surplus generation — typically after a system separation where an island keeps more generation than load. The response is generator runback or tripping, not load restoration.
  • Islanded operation is where both directions matter at once: a small island with local generation swings quickly in either direction, and the frequency elements are doing real control work rather than emergency backstop.
  • Off-frequency accumulators on generators track cumulative time spent in bands where turbine blades fatigue — a separate concern from system stability. See Generator Protection.

Test the scheme end to end at least once. Inject a declining frequency ramp into the relay and watch the stages operate in order, with the right delays, opening the right breakers. A scheme verified only element-by-element on the bench has never been proven as a scheme — and it may be years before a real event tests it for you.

7Gotchas

  • Undervoltage block set wrong or disabled. The single most common cause of spurious load shedding during a nearby fault.
  • Frequency measured from one phase. A single blown PT fuse takes out the whole scheme. Alarm the loss of potential.
  • All stages on the same relay and the same breaker. Staging means nothing if every stage sheds the same load.
  • Stage timers too long. A cascade that takes eight seconds to complete has already lost the race against the generators' own underfrequency trips.
  • Instantaneous elements left in service after testing. They will shed on a transient.
  • No plan for restoration. After a real event, operators need a documented, staggered restoration order. Writing it during the event does not go well.
  • Settings copied from an old file without checking the current regional requirement, or without accounting for changed system inertia.
  • The scheme disarmed for testing and left disarmed. Make the armed state a SCADA point with an alarm on disarm.
