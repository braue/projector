☰The rules, stated plainly

Generators

  • Opening the breaker does not de-energize a generator. The machine keeps spinning with field applied and keeps feeding an internal fault. Tripping a generator means the breaker and the field and the prime mover.
  • Most generator elements protect the machine from the system, not the system from the machine: reverse power, off-frequency, overexcitation, unbalance, and loss of field are all abnormal-operating-condition protection.
  • Fault current decays below rated current. A plain overcurrent element can pick up and drop out before timing. Backup overcurrent on a generator is voltage-controlled or voltage-restrained (51V), or a compensator distance element.
  • The rotor is fragile in ways the stator is not. Negative-sequence current, loss of field, and field grounds all attack it, and none of them look like a fault.
  • Ground current is deliberately tiny. Generators are usually high-impedance grounded, so stator ground protection is voltage-based, and covering the last few percent near the neutral needs a dedicated 100% stator ground scheme.
  • Synchronizing is protection. Closing out of phase can wreck a machine instantly — sync check is a hard interlock, not a convenience.

Why stator ground protection is voltage-based. Generators are almost always high-impedance grounded — commonly through a distribution transformer with a secondary resistor — so a solid stator ground draws only a few amperes. That protects the iron from burning, but it means current-based ground elements have nothing to measure. 59N on the neutral (or a broken-delta 3V0) is the primary detector. See System Grounding.

3Why generator backup overcurrent needs voltage

A synchronous generator's contribution to a sustained fault decays from the subtransient value, through the transient value, down to the synchronous value — which for many machines is below rated current. A conventional 51 element set above rated current will pick up on the first cycles and then drop out before it times out.

This is the single most distinctive thing about generator protection, and the reason a feeder relay cannot simply be repurposed onto a machine. (Animated in the browser.)

5Synchronizing

Paralleling a machine to a live system requires voltage magnitude, frequency, and phase angle to match within tight limits. Closing out of step subjects the shaft to torque that can exceed a three-phase fault.

  • 25 — synchronism check is a permissive: it allows a close only when slip, angle, and voltage difference are all within limits. On the SEL-700G family there are separate generator sync-check and tie sync-check elements, plus sync-check under- and overvoltage elements.
  • Autosynchronism goes further: the relay actively issues raise/lower commands to the governor and exciter to bring the machine into alignment, then closes at the correct advance angle to account for breaker closing time.
  • Synchroscope gives the operator the classic visual. The SEL-700G family exposes synchroscope and auto-synchronizer applications on the touchscreen display.
  • Breaker close time must be compensated. Issuing the close at the moment of zero angle means the breaker actually closes late, at a real angle difference. The advance-angle setting handles this, and it is a per-breaker measured value — not a guess.
Test the sync-check element for what it blocks, not just what it permits. A sync element that is wired backwards, or whose voltage inputs are swapped, will happily permit closes that should be blocked, and nothing about normal operation will reveal it. Prove the block with a deliberate angle and voltage mismatch during commissioning. See Synchronism Check & Closing.

6Intertie protection and islanding

When a generator is embedded in a distribution or industrial system, a second problem appears: the machine must not continue to energize a section that has been separated from the utility. The SEL-700GT is the intertie member of the family, and the SEL-700GW targets wind generation.

  • Vector shift and rate of change of frequency (81R) are the standard islanding detectors, both available on the SEL-700G family. Both react to the sudden change in the balance between generation and load at the instant of separation.
  • Directional power (32) enforces export or import limits at the point of interconnection.
  • 27/59 and 81 O/U ride-through settings come from the interconnection agreement and the applicable standard, not from machine data — and they have changed substantially as ride-through requirements have tightened. See DER Interconnection.
  • Transfer trip remains the most dependable anti-islanding method where a channel exists, because it does not depend on detecting a subtle system change.

7Tripping schemes

Generator tripping is not one action. The scheme names describe which combination of breaker, field, and prime mover is commanded, and picking the wrong one for a given fault causes secondary damage.

SchemeActionsUsed for
Simultaneous (unit) tripGenerator breaker, field breaker, and prime mover all at onceInternal faults — anything where continued rotation with excitation makes the damage worse (87, 64G, field ground with a second ground)
Generator tripBreaker and field, prime mover left running (or ramped)Electrical abnormal conditions where the machine itself is intact
Sequential tripClose the prime mover valves first, wait for reverse power to confirm the machine is unloaded, then open the breaker and fieldNormal shutdowns and non-urgent trips on steam units — avoids overspeed by ensuring the machine is not still driven when the load is removed
System (breaker only) tripGenerator breaker onlySystem-side conditions where the machine can survive on house load
The lockout relay is doing more here than anywhere else. A generator 86 typically drives the breaker, the field breaker, the turbine trip solenoid, and the auxiliaries. When you map generator trip points into SCADA, map the lockout and the individual initiating elements separately — “86G operated” is the operational fact, and “which element caused it” is the engineering fact. Both are needed.

8Gotchas

  • Applying a feeder relay to a generator. Without 51V or 21 backup, the machine has no working backup overcurrent protection at all — and nothing about normal operation reveals it.
  • Loss of potential silently disabling protection. A blown VT fuse blocks 51V, 40, 32, and 21 by design. That block must raise an alarm, and the alarm must be treated as an urgent protection outage.
  • Differential CTs with mismatched ratios or saturation characteristics. Neutral-end and terminal-end CTs are often different physical types. Confirm both against the differential relay's requirements, and use the high-security mode where external-event saturation is credible.
  • Field ground module wiring. The SEL-2664 connects to the field circuit; getting its connection or its blocking logic wrong produces either constant alarms or no detection. Prove it with a deliberate test ground during commissioning.
  • Off-frequency accumulators are cumulative across the machine's life in some schemes. Understand whether the relay's accumulator is meant to be reset, and by whom.
  • Inadvertent energization logic must be armed and disarmed correctly. Armed when it should not be, it can trip a healthy start; disarmed when it should be armed, it protects nothing.
  • Synchronizing across the wrong pair of PTs. Running-side and incoming-side voltage inputs are easy to reverse, and normal parallel operation will not reveal it. Verify with a real angle difference.
