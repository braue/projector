Differential Protection (87)

Kirchhoff on a zone — operate vs. restraint, the slope characteristic, TAP and connection compensation, inrush harmonics, and why delta-wye gets people

☰The rules, stated plainly

  • Everything flowing into a CT-bounded zone must flow out. If the phasor sum isn't ~zero, current is disappearing inside the zone — a fault.
  • The relay trips on operate current vs. restraint current: a slope characteristic tolerates the measurement error that grows with through current.
  • Transformers break the raw sum three ways — ratio, phase shift, zero sequence. TAP scaling fixes ratio; connection compensation (WnCTC / TRCON) fixes phase shift and strips zero sequence. Get compensation wrong and the relay trips on external ground faults.
  • Energization inrush looks like an internal fault. Second-harmonic content is how the relay tells the difference.
  • Commission with load: MET DIF should show IOP near zero. Standing operate current = wiring or settings error, not a fault.

1The principle: a zone, and Kirchhoff

Draw a boundary through a set of CTs around the thing you protect. Sum every measured current with a reference direction into the zone. Healthy or externally faulted, the sum is ~zero; internally faulted, it isn't. The SEL-387 has three differential elements (87-1, 87-2, 87-3 — one per phase) and computes two quantities per element from the (compensated, TAP-scaled) winding currents:

  • IOP (operate) — the magnitude of the phasor sum of the winding currents. Through current at rated load: about 1 + (−1) = 0.
  • IRT (restraint) — the sum of the current magnitudes, divided by two. Through current at rated load: (|1| + |−1|)/2 = 1.

Both are in dimensionless multiples of TAP, so "1" means rated transformer current regardless of winding. IOP measures how badly Kirchhoff is violated; IRT measures how much current is flowing through, i.e., how much error you should tolerate.

One zone, three situations. Through current sums to zero; an internal fault doesn't; inrush doesn't either — so the relay checks harmonic content before believing the differential current. ▶ Animated in the HTML edition.

2The percentage-restraint slope characteristic

A flat IOP threshold can't work: CT ratio error, LTC position, and CT saturation all produce false operate current that grows with through current. The 387's worked example: ±10% CT error plus a ±10% LTC swing gives up to 28.18% apparent differential current on a purely external event; add ~3% excitation current and 5% relay error and the total error budget is 36%. So the trip boundary is a slope in the IOP–IRT plane — tolerated error rises with restraint. Tripping requires IOP above the curve and above the minimum pickup O87P.

The 387 manual notes the dual-slope characteristic exists to absorb "CT ratio mismatches, CT ratio errors, CT saturation, and errors because of tap changing."

3Matching the transformer: TAP, MVA, and ratios

Winding currents arrive at the relay in different secondary amperes. TAP scaling normalizes them to per-unit of transformer rating so they can cancel. Per winding n (SEL-387):

TAPn = (MVA × 1000) / (√3 × VWDGn × CTRn) × C   ·   C = 1 wye CTs, √3 delta CTs
  • MVA (range OFF, 0.2–5000): set to the highest expected rating (FOA or emergency). With MVA set, the relay calculates TAP1–TAP4 itself; MVA = OFF lets you enter TAPn directly (5 A relay: 0.5–155 A range).
  • VWDGn: nominal line-to-line kV. If an LTC is inside the zone, assume neutral position — the slope absorbs the swing either side.
  • WnCT = D (delta CTs) raises TAPn by 1.732.
  • TAPMAX/TAPMIN must be ≤ 7.5 — the relay checks and flags a violation; fix it with CT ratio choices. (387 example: 10.46/1.74 = 6.01, OK.)

4Connection compensation — the classic trap

A delta-wye transformer shifts current 30° and, on the grounded-wye side, sources zero-sequence current for external ground faults that the delta side never sees. Old practice fixed both by wiring the wye-side CTs in delta. Modern practice wires all CTs in wye and lets the relay do the math — but then you must tell it the math.

SEL-387: ICOM and WnCTC

Set ICOM = Y, then W1CTC–W4CTC pick a 3×3 compensation matrix CTC(0)–CTC(12): each step is 30° of rotation (CCW for ABC rotation, CW for ACB). CTC(0) is the identity — no shift, no zero-sequence removal. Every non-zero matrix, including CTC(12) (360° = 0° shift), removes zero-sequence from that winding's currents. The manual's recipe:

1 · Get the drawingstransformer nameplate + three-line showing system-phase→bushing, CT, and CT→relay connections
→
2 · Pick the referencea delta winding if one is wired in → CTC(0). No delta → CTC(11) on one wye winding
→
3 · Rotate the restset the other windings' shift relative to the reference; odd matrices for wye windings, avoid even ones where possible

Worked default (230/138 kV wye autotransformer + 13.8 kV delta tertiary, all wye CTs, IEEE-standard 30° lag): delta on W3 is the reference, W3CTC = 0; the wye currents need 330° CCW, so W1CTC = W2CTC = 11.

The zero-sequence rule (from the 387 commissioning sheet): a grounded-wye winding with wye CTs must never sit at WnCTC = 0 — use 12 instead, purely for the zero-sequence filtering. Load checks will not catch this mistake; the first external phase-to-ground fault will, as a false differential trip.

SEL-587: TRCON (AG2000-01)

The 587 asks for the connection by name instead of by matrix: TRCON = DABY or DACY (plus CTCON = YY for wye CTs). AG2000-01's method — and its traps:

  • From the three-line, find which bushing (H1/H2/H3) carries system A-phase. That bushing is "the polarity of A-phase."
  • From the nameplate, see whether A-phase's polarity connects to the non-polarity of B-phase (→ DAB) or of C-phase (→ DAC).
  • Phase rotation does not change TRCON. ABC vs. ACB flips whether the high side leads or lags by 30°, but the same nameplate + same landings give the same setting; set PHROT to the system rotation separately.
  • Identical nameplates can need different settings: land C-B-A on H1-H2-H3 instead of A-B-C and DABY becomes DACY. The nameplate alone is not enough — you need the system connection diagram.

AG2000-01's standing recommendations: wye CTs everywhere (residual elements stay available, secondary currents aren't √3-inflated, wiring is simpler), and always land system A/B/C on relay A/B/C.

Blocking (HRSTR = N)

Each harmonic is an independent go/no-go test: if I2/I1 > PCT2 or I4/I1 > PCT4 (or 5th > PCT5, or dc ratio), the blocking element 87BLn picks up and vetoes the restrained element. IHBL picks the scope: N = common (cross) blocking — any phase blocks all elements, more secure, slightly slower reset; Y = per-phase blocking, mainly for banks of single-phase units with independent-pole breakers.

Restraint (HRSTR = Y, not -387-0)

The scaled harmonics raise the characteristic instead of vetoing it: the sum (100/PCT2)·I2 + (100/PCT4)·I4 becomes the constant c in IOP = SLP1·IRT + c — the more harmonic content, the higher the trip boundary. Always per-phase; IHBL is ignored. More secure than blocking. Do not set PCT2/PCT4 to OFF with HRSTR = Y — that silently removes all harmonic restraint. 5th-harmonic blocking stays independent of HRSTR.

687U — the unrestrained high-set

Elements 87U1–3 compare IOP directly against U87P (range 1.0–20.0 × TAP, set ~10 × TAP) with no slope, no harmonic blocking, no restraint — nothing is allowed to slow it down. Fundamental-frequency component only. Its job is the currents that "clearly indicate an internal fault" — bushing and end-winding faults — so it must sit above worst-case inrush, since harmonic blocking won't save it. Both 87R and 87U are high-speed elements that should trip all zone breakers, classically via an 86 lockout.

7Restricted earth fault (REF)

The phase differential element has little sensitivity for wye-winding ground faults near the neutral — fault current shrinks with the fraction of shorted winding. REF compares zero-sequence current at the winding terminals against the neutral CT current, restricting protection to ground faults between those CTs, sensitive down toward the first turn from neutral (487E wording).

  • 387 implementation: a directional element (32I). Polarizing current = neutral CT (wired into one Winding-4 single-phase input) × CTR4; operating current = phasor sum of the selected windings' residuals × their CTRs, all in primary amperes (32IOP selects the windings — covers a single wye winding or a whole autotransformer with up to three line-end CT sets). The element computes Re(IOP·IPOL*): positive (angle within ±90°) = internal → 32IF after 1.5 cycles; negative = external → 32IR. Pickup 50GP on the neutral current enables it; a second path trips on neutral current with no line-end current (fault with the breaker open).
  • Wye line CTs are mandatory — delta CTs cancel the zero-sequence quantity REF compares.
  • Security: a positive-sequence restraint blocks the element when a winding residual looks like "false I0" from CT saturation on heavy three-phase faults (CTS bit; supervise with E32I = !CTS). Tripping can be direct from 32IF or delayed via an extremely-inverse U4 curve (TD 0.5) → REFP.
  • The 487E carries REF forward with up to three independent REF elements.

8The modern flavor: what the SEL-487E adds

  • Adaptive slope instead of a fixed dual slope. The characteristic is a single line through the origin, IOP = k·SLPc·IRT. Dedicated external fault detection logic (a change in restraint without matching operate current) switches the relay within 2 ms into high-security mode: Slope 1 is swapped for Slope 2 and the adaptive security timer stretches. It does not block for external faults — so an evolving external→internal fault still trips, just against the steeper slope. High-security holds ≥3 cycles, resets after 60 cycles or when CT-unsaturate logic says the danger is past.
  • Slope arithmetic differs: k = 1 in the 487E vs. k = 0.5 in the SEL-387/587 restraint calculation — the manual explicitly warns to account for this when carrying slope settings across.
  • Negative-sequence differential (87Q) for turn-to-turn faults under load: balanced load contributes no negative sequence, so 87Q operates at ~0.1 pu operate current where the phase element (loaded) needed ~0.43 pu in the manual's 2%-of-winding example. Restraint is the maximum winding negative-sequence current; supervised by the external-fault detector (CON) and harmonic blocking (87QB), with a recommended 10-cycle delay for security.
  • Harmonic blocking and restraint in parallel — you can enable both (blocking is always cross-phase, restraint always independent-phase); SEL calls the combination a good balance of speed and security.
  • Big CT ratio mismatch tolerated — up to 25:1 (e.g., 2000/5 and 80/5 in a breaker-and-a-half bay), with per-input 1 A/5 A ordering.

9Other 87 zones, briefly

87B — bus differential

Same Kirchhoff principle, no transformer in the zone — so no TAP/phase-shift compensation, just one zone spanning every breaker on the bus. The defining challenge is security: a close-in external fault drives huge through current into one saturating CT, manufacturing false operate current, and bus trips drop every feeder at once. Bus schemes are therefore built around CT-saturation security (restraint from every terminal, or the high-impedance approach that lets a saturated CT's low impedance shunt the operate current), and zones must follow bus sectionalizing.

87L — line differential

The zone's CTs are kilometers apart, so each terminal's relay sends its measured currents to the far end over a communications channel and both compare. That makes the scheme unit protection with no reach setting to coordinate — but it inherits the channel's problems: the currents must be time-aligned (channel delay measurement or external time reference), asymmetric channel delay skews the comparison, and long lines add charging current that appears differential. Loss of channel means loss of the scheme, so backup elements ride along.

10Commissioning: prove the zone with load

The 387's commissioning sheet is a checklist for exactly the errors that produce false trips later. It wants real load — >0.1 A secondary, 0.5 A recommended — because with load current flowing, "the correctness (or lack thereof) of all the input connections becomes apparent."

  • MET DIF: read IOP1–3 and IRT1–3 (multiples of TAP) live. Compute mismatch MMn = IOPn/IRTn; it must be < 0.10. Anything more = standing operate current: check magnitudes, angles, and an event report. (Arithmetic worth memorizing: a rolled CT polarity makes that winding add instead of cancel — IOP lands near 2× the through current instead of ~0; a wrong ratio or TAP shows as IOP proportional to the ratio error.)
  • MET SEC: per-winding magnitudes, angles, and sequence currents. Verify calculated vs. metered amperes, phase rotation per winding, and that windings' angular relationships make sense — current flowing out of a winding reads 180° from the reference because CT polarity marks normally face away from the transformer.
  • Zero-sequence filtering check (settings review, §4): no grounded-wye winding with wye CTs on WnCTC = 0. The load checks above cannot detect this one.
  • MET DIF also shows the 2nd/5th-harmonic element currents — the same ratios (×100 vs. PCT2/PCT5) the blocking logic uses, handy when chasing a blocked element during energization.
  • Cross-check panel meters (MW/MVAR → MVA → primary and secondary amperes per winding) against the relay before trusting any of it.
Field rule: a differential that alarms or trips at energization or under first load has a settings/wiring problem until proven otherwise — compensation (§4), TAP inputs (§3), or CT polarity. The event report's per-winding currents plus MET DIF will point at the winding.
