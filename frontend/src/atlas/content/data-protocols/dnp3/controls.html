☰The rules, stated plainly

DNP3 in general

  • A control is a message, not a level. The master sends it once; the outstation acts on it once.
  • The control code says what you want. The outstation's control model decides what actually happens.
  • Latch on/off sets and clears a state. Pulse fires a momentary action.
  • Trip and close both pulse — TRIP momentarily energizes the trip coil, CLOSE the close coil, on the same point. Coils are never latched; the breaker mechanism holds the position.
  • SUCCESS means "command accepted," not "breaker moved." Proof comes back on the binary input.

1One message, two decisions

In DNP3 (IEEE 1815), a master never toggles an output directly. It sends a Control Relay Output Block (CROB, Group 12 Var 1) addressed to a binary output point, and the physical outcome is decided in two places:

  • The master decides the operation — the control code inside the CROB.
  • The outstation decides the behavior — each point's control model, configured at the device, defines how that code drives hardware.

Everything else in this document is the detail of those two decisions: what's in the message, what the three models do with it, and what each end of an RTAC-to-relay link actually executes.

Control code bits: TCC (bits 7–6): 00 NUL · 01 CLOSE · 10 TRIP  ·  Op type (bits 3–0): 0 NUL · 1 PULSE_ON · 2 PULSE_OFF · 3 LATCH_ON · 4 LATCH_OFF. Bits 5 (Clear) and 4 (Queue) are legacy — normally 0.

The point behaves like a maintained relay: latch-on sets it, latch-off clears it, and it stays there until commanded otherwise (or the device restarts). Timing fields are ignored. Use it for enable/disable, mode selection, blocking an automatic scheme.

One DNP point, two physical outputs — typically a breaker's trip and close coil. The TCC bits pick which coil gets the pulse; the coils are only ever pulsed, and the breaker mechanism holds the position. Trip and close share one index — mapping them to two indices is a classic integration mistake.

3Delivery: SBO or direct

Whatever the operation, the CROB can be delivered two ways. The CROB contents are identical — only the handshake differs. On the RTAC client this is the session-level Output Control Type setting.

Select-Before-Operate (SBO)

Two-pass arm-then-fire: SELECT (FC 3) + CROB, outstation echoes SUCCESS to arm the point, then OPERATE (FC 4) with the identical CROB inside the select window. Wrong CROB, wrong point, or too late → NO_SELECT / TIMEOUT. The default for switching — the select proves the right point is armed before anything moves.

Select windows are short (commonly 5–30 s). An operator pausing between select and operate is the usual cause of TIMEOUT.

▶ Animated in the HTML edition.

Direct Operate

FC 5 — one pass: execute immediately, reply with status. Fine for non-critical or frequently repeated controls (raise/lower).

FC 6 (no-ack) — execute, no response at all. Broadcast / fire-and-forget only.

5Who actually owns the pulse width

The single most confusing thing in the field: the RTAC says 2000 ms, so why does the remote bit blink for 2 ms? Because every stage of the chain has its own timing authority, and the CROB's on-time survives exactly one hop:

1 · Your logicoperPulse.ctlVal := TRUE — edge-triggered; hold time is irrelevant
→
2 · RTAC clientbuilds CROB with count=1, on=2000 ms, off=2000 ms (per-point settings)
→
3 · SEL relayignores count/on/off — pulses the bit for one processing interval (~2–4 ms)
→
4 · Relay logicSELOGIC catches the edge; TRIP logic + TDUR3D stretch the contact (9 cyc default on a 451)

One breaker-open command, end to end. The 2000 ms in the CROB dies at hop 3; the contact time comes from the relay's own logic. ▶ Animated in the HTML edition.

From the SEL-400 series and SEL-751A manuals, verbatim: "The status field is used exactly as defined. All other fields are ignored. A pulse operation is asserted for a single processing interval." A 451 processes 8 times per cycle (~2.1 ms at 60 Hz); a 751A processes 4 times per cycle (~4.2 ms). The RTAC's On Pulse Duration is faithfully written into the CROB — and then discarded by the relay.

Three consequences follow:

  • Never expect a DNP pulse to hold anything in an SEL relay. A pulsed remote bit exists to be caught — by SELOGIC rising-edge operators (/RB1, latch set/reset equations) or by the OC/CC → TRIP/CLOSE logic. For a maintained state, use latch on/off, not a long on-time.
  • The physical contact duration comes from the relay: minimum trip duration timers (TDUR3D, 451 default 9 cycles), seal-in conditions, and close-logic supervision — not the SCADA message.
  • The asymmetry cuts both ways. When the RTAC is the outstation, its contact outputs do execute configured pulse timing (see DNP3 on the RTAC) — so the same CROB produces a 2 ms blip on a relay and a 2000 ms closure on an RTAC contact, unless you align the settings.

The device ends of the wire are covered in DNP3 on the RTAC and DNP3 on SEL Relays in atlas.

6Troubleshooting & fine print

  • Count = 0 means do nothing per IEEE 1815 (some older devices treat it as 1 — rely on neither).
  • Interoperability fallback: IEEE 1815 recommends two-output points also accept LATCH_ON as close and LATCH_OFF as trip (the RTAC's auto-generated CommProc maps use exactly this) — but prefer the proper trip/close codes.
  • Analog setpoints are different objects entirely (Group 41). Everything here is binary outputs (Group 10/12) only.
