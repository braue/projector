☰The rules, stated plainly

SER and SOE

  • SER logs digital state changes as they happen — 1 ms tags, nonvolatile, no trigger and no window. It is running right now on every relay you own.
  • Fast SER pushes those entries to the RTAC live; the RTAC's merged, system-wide log is the SOE. The SER command is the after-the-fact pull.
  • Ordering events across devices is only as good as the time sync. Without a common IRIG-B or PTP source, a cross-device sequence is a guess.
  • The trigger list is a design decision. Defaults are rarely what you want; a bit you did not select is a bit that was never recorded.
  • SER answers “what happened first?”; the event report answers “what did it look like?” You need both, and they are separate records.
  • SOE is not the alarm list. Alarms are for operators to act on; SOE is the forensic record. Designing one to do the other's job fails at both.

1SER — the relay's own logbook

One row per digital state change, time-tagged to the millisecond with about ±¼-cycle accuracy, written to nonvolatile memory as the change happens. No trigger to arm, no capture window to miss, and no dependence on anyone having asked for it.

  • You choose which Relay Word bits are monitored — up to 96 on the SEL-751A. The SEL-451 keeps the last 1000 entries.
  • Some things are logged automatically: power-ups, settings changes, and settings-group changes. That alone makes the SER the first place to look when a device “started behaving differently.”
  • Settings via SET R; retrieval via SER, or CSER for a comma-delimited form that imports cleanly into a spreadsheet.
  • It survives. Nonvolatile means a power cycle does not lose it — unlike the momentary state you were trying to observe.

The event report shows the waveforms; the SER shows the sequence. Together they answer “what happened” and “in what order.” (Animated in the browser.)

2Choosing what to trigger on

An SER slot spent on a bit that never changes is wasted; a bit you needed and did not select is gone forever. The list is worth designing deliberately, and it belongs in the settings template so every relay of a type records the same things.

Always TRIP and CLOSE · every element that appears in the trip equation, both pickup and timeout · 52A / 52B · lockout (86) · the relay's own alarm and self-test bits
Nearly always Reclose state bits (reset, in-progress, lockout, shot counter) · loss of potential · settings-group indication · local/remote switch · time-source status
Scheme-dependent Received and transmitted Mirrored Bits or GOOSE bits · breaker-failure initiate and timeout · permissives and blocks · interlock inputs · the outputs of any custom SELogic you wrote
Worth adding The inputs and outputs that go to other equipment — when the neighbouring device misbehaves, your SER is the evidence about what you sent it
Usually skip Bits that chatter with load or measurement noise · pickup bits of elements that pick up routinely · anything that would fill the buffer in normal operation
Fix, do not filter A genuinely chattering input should be debounced at the relay input, not excluded from the SER. Excluding it hides the problem
A full buffer overwrites the oldest entries. On a relay whose SER is filling with routine chatter, the fault you actually care about has already been pushed out by the time anyone looks. Check buffer utilization during commissioning, not after an event.

3From SER to SOE

SER is each relay's private log. SOE — sequence of events — is the same kind of data assembled system-wide: the RTAC or the SCADA master collects every device's state changes into one merged, time-ordered record.

Coordination is proven, or disproven, in this merged list. It is the single most valuable artifact after a misoperation. (Animated in the browser.)

Give the bits names. The SNS point-name strings are what turn a Fast SER stream from “bit 47 changed” into “FDR1 breaker opened.” Setting them during commissioning costs minutes; not setting them costs every future investigation.

4Timestamps and what they are worth

  • SER entries are tagged to 1 ms with about ±¼-cycle accuracy. Ordering events across devices is only meaningful when everything shares IRIG-B or PTP.
  • The 400-series tags COMTRADE samples to better than 10 µs — for exactly this reason.
  • Check the time-source status bit, not just that a cable is present. A relay in holdover is still producing timestamps; they are simply drifting. See Time Synchronization.
  • Protocol timestamps are a different thing. On DNP3, points registered in the SER carry true device time; everything else carries scan time. Two points in the same SCADA event list can therefore have very different accuracy — see DNP3 on SEL Relays.
  • Record the time source in the investigation. “All devices were IRIG-locked at the time” is a sentence that makes a conclusion defensible.

5Using it

  1. Pull the SER before doing anything else when investigating. It is nonvolatile, but a settings change can clear buffers on some relays.
  2. Export with CSER and open it in a spreadsheet if you need to sort, filter, or merge with another device's log by hand.
  3. Build the timeline first, then look at waveforms. The SER tells you which event report to open and what to look for in it — see Event Analysis.
  4. Look for what did not happen. An element that should have picked up and did not is often the finding, and only the SER shows an absence.
  5. Trend the intervals. Trip-to-52A across many operations is a breaker health measurement hiding in the log.
  6. Check settings-change entries when a device “started behaving differently.” They are logged automatically, and they are frequently the answer.

6Troubleshooting & fine print

  • Do not order cross-device events on unsynchronized clocks. Before trusting a system-wide sequence, confirm every device in it had IRIG-B or PTP at the time.
  • The bit you needed was not in the trigger list. The most common disappointment. Review and extend the list after every investigation that found a gap.
  • The buffer had already wrapped. Chattering bits fill it. Fix the chatter with input debounce rather than removing the point.
  • Fast SER stream missing names → SNS strings were never configured, so the SOE is full of unnamed bits.
  • SOE and the alarm list disagree. They should — the alarm list is filtered and prioritized for operators, the SOE is complete. Trust the SOE for forensics.
  • The RTAC's SOE only has what it was told to collect. A device that is polled but whose Fast SER is not enabled contributes only what the protocol map exposes.
  • Retention. Know how long the SOE is kept and where it is archived before you need a record from six months ago.
