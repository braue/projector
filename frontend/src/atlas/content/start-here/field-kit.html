☰The rules, stated plainly

Working in the field

  • Pull before you push. The first thing you do on any device is read and save its present state — settings, firmware ID, event history. If the visit goes wrong, that file is your way back.
  • One change at a time, and know how to undo it. Two simultaneous changes turn a five-minute diagnosis into an afternoon.
  • Never trust a cable you did not prove. Serial adapters, null-modems, and mystery patch cords cause more “dead device” calls than dead devices do.
  • Assume the panel is live. CT secondaries, trip circuits, and station DC are all present on terminals that look identical to the ones that are not — see Safety Practices.
  • Write it down while you are standing there. Terminal numbers, as-found switch positions, and odd noises never survive the drive home.

1What lives in the bag

Connectivity

  • SEL-C662 (USB–to–DB9 serial cable for SEL front ports) plus a spare; a plain USB–serial adapter and a DB9 null-modem and gender changer for everything else.
  • Ethernet patch cords, short and long, plus a small unmanaged switch (invaluable for sniffing and for talking to two devices at once).
  • SFP spares matching the site's optics, and LC/ST/SC fiber jumpers plus a fiber cleaning pen.
  • USB stick (for RTAC/relay file transfer where permitted by policy) and a USB–C hub — modern laptops have no serial and often one port.

Measurement

  • True-RMS DMM with good leads; clamp meter that reads AC amps down to a few amps (for proving CT secondaries and load).
  • Phase rotation meter (or a relay that reports it) for proving PT wiring.
  • Insulated screwdrivers sized for the terminal blocks you will actually meet, and a shorting jumper set for CT circuits.

Paper and light

  • Printed one-line, three-line, AC/DC elementaries, panel schedule, and the points list. Paper works when the laptop battery does not, and you can red-line it.
  • Headlamp. Substations at 4 p.m. in December are dark places, and both hands will be busy.
  • Labels and a label printer. An unlabeled temporary jumper is a future outage.

Safety

  • Arc-rated clothing appropriate to the panel's incident-energy label, safety glasses, and voltage-rated gloves if the task calls for them.
  • Lockout/tagout kit, and your own set of locks.
The two items people forget: a spare serial cable, and a way to charge the laptop. Both stop a site visit dead.

3Four doors into a device

Every SEL device offers several ways in, with different privileges and different risk. Knowing which door to knock on — and which one your site's security policy permits — saves an hour per visit.

Access level is a property of the session, not of the port. Level 2 over a serial cable in the panel and Level 2 through the gateway can do identical damage. (Animated in the browser.)

DoorUse it whenWatch out for
Front panelReading targets, checking metering, resetting a lockout, verifying an IP without a laptopSome settings are hidden or read-only from the panel; panel access may be password-protected separately

5File hygiene

A year from now, the only thing standing between you and a bad afternoon is whether these files can be found and trusted.

Name by fact, not by mood SITE_DEVICE_YYYYMMDD_asfound.rdb beats settings_final_v2_REAL.rdb. Site, device tag, ISO date, and the words asfound or asleft.
Always keep the pair Every visit produces an as-found and an as-left, even when they are identical. “Nothing changed” is a finding worth proving.
Archive off the laptop Before you leave the parking lot, copy the day's files to wherever your organization keeps the record of truth. Laptops get stolen, dropped, and reimaged.
Note the why A one-line change note (“raised 51P TD from 2.0 to 2.5 for coordination with new recloser R-14”) is worth more than the diff itself.
Keep the event files COMTRADE records of anything interesting, named the same way. They are the only evidence that survives a firmware upgrade.
Red-line the paper Photograph your marked-up drawings at the end of the day. Red-lines that never become as-builts are how sites drift out of documentation.

The longer-term discipline — templates, version control, and settings review — is in Settings Management.

6Before you leave site

The walk-back list

  • Every temporary jumper, shorting screw, and test lead removed — verified by touch, against the list you wrote when you installed them.
  • Every test switch and control switch back to its as-found position; photograph the switch bank.
  • Relay targets reset, self-test healthy, no standing alarms that were not there when you arrived.
  • Trip circuits restored and, where practical, proven (trip-coil monitoring bit asserted).
  • SCADA confirms the device is online and reporting sane values — call the operator and ask, do not assume.

The record

  • As-left settings read back from the device and saved — not the file you intended to send.
  • Test sheets signed; punch list of anything left undone, with owners.
  • Red-lines photographed; changes reported to whoever maintains the drawings.
  • Passwords changed off defaults and recorded in the sanctioned place, never on a sticky note.
  • Panel doors closed and secured; cabinet keys returned.
The one that bites hardest: a CT shorting screw left in. The relay reads zero current, coordinates with nothing, and nobody finds out until the next fault. Count them out loud, and have a second person verify.

7Habits that separate good from lucky

  • Narrate your assumptions out loud. “I am assuming this terminal block is the trip circuit” is the sentence that gets corrected before it becomes an incident.
  • Prove direction, not just presence. A voltage reading proves a wire is energized; it does not prove which wire.
  • When something surprises you, stop and understand it. Surprises are the system telling you your model is wrong. Continuing with a wrong model is how misoperations are built.
  • Take the extra event capture. Storage is free; a second trip to site is not.
  • Ask the operator what they see. They live with these screens; they will tell you in ten seconds which point has always looked wrong.
  • Leave the site better labeled than you found it. The next person may be you.
