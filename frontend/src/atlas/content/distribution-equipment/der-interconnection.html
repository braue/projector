Distributed generation

  • A radial feeder stops being radial. Power can flow either way, fault current can arrive from downstream, and every protection assumption built on “one source at the substation” needs re-examining.
  • Islanding is the safety problem. A DER that keeps energizing a disconnected section endangers line crews, defeats reclosing, and can reconnect out of synchronism.
  • Inverters are not synchronous machines. They are current-limited — commonly barely above rated current — and their fault-current angle is whatever the control chooses. Classical directional and negative-sequence assumptions weaken.
  • Ride-through and anti-islanding pull in opposite directions. Modern interconnection rules require DER to stay connected through disturbances that older rules required them to trip for. Know which requirement applies.
  • Grounding at the point of interconnection is not a detail. The transformer connection decides whether an islanded section stays effectively grounded — which decides whether arresters survive.
  • Transfer trip is the dependable answer where a channel exists, because it does not rely on detecting a subtle system change.

1What changes on the feeder

The infeed effect is the subtle one: a device between the substation and the fault sees less current than the study predicted, because part of the fault current now comes from the other direction. (Animated in the browser.)

2Inverter fault behavior

A synchronous generator is a voltage source behind an impedance, and its fault contribution is set by physics. An inverter is a controlled current source, and its fault contribution is set by firmware.

Synchronous machineInverter-based resource
Fault current magnitudeSeveral times rated, decaying over cycles to secondsCurrent-limited by control, commonly only slightly above rated
Fault current angleSet by machine and network impedance — strongly inductiveChosen by the control algorithm; may be near unity, or deliberately reactive to support voltage
Negative-sequence contributionSubstantial and predictableDepends entirely on control strategy — some inverters suppress it deliberately
InertiaReal rotating massNone inherently; synthetic inertia only if implemented
Behavior during a deep sagContinues, governed by physicsGoverned by the ride-through requirement it was certified to
Do not size protection from a textbook assumption about DER contribution. Read the interconnection study and the manufacturer's fault-current characteristic. An element that relies on negative-sequence current or on a conventional fault angle may simply not see an inverter-fed fault — and the study is the only place that fact is documented.

3Islanding, and how it is detected

An island forms when a section separates from the utility while local generation happens to roughly match local load. If generation and load matched perfectly, nothing about voltage or frequency would change — which is why detection methods look for the mismatch, and why a “non-detection zone” exists in principle for every passive method.

MethodHow it worksStrengths and limits
Under/overvoltage and under/overfrequency (27/59, 81U/81O)The island's voltage or frequency drifts outside the windowUniversal, simple, and required. Blind when generation and load are closely matched — and increasingly constrained by ride-through requirements
Rate of change of frequency (81R / ROCOF)The instant of separation produces a frequency slope proportional to the mismatchFast. Can be fooled by system events, faults, and motor starting; less reliable as system inertia falls
Vector shiftThe sudden change in load causes a step in voltage phase angleFast and sensitive; also susceptible to nearby faults and switching
Active methods (inverter-resident)The inverter injects a small perturbation and watches whether the grid absorbs itVery effective for a single inverter; multiple inverters can interfere with each other's methods
Direct transfer tripThe utility device that opened tells the DER to disconnect over a communications channelThe dependable method. No non-detection zone. Costs a channel and its maintenance — see Mirrored Bits and Radio & Wide-Area Links
Anti-islanding is a crew-safety requirement first. A line worker's protection is grounding and verification, not a relay setting — but the relay setting is what keeps the situation from arising. Treat anti-islanding function tests as safety-critical, and prove them, not just the settings.

4Ride-through versus trip

Early interconnection requirements told DER to disconnect quickly for any disturbance. That was fine when DER was a rounding error. As penetration grew, a voltage sag that tripped every inverter in a region became a bigger problem than the original disturbance — so requirements shifted to ride-through: stay connected, and support the grid, through defined voltage and frequency excursions.

  • The two requirements genuinely conflict. Ride-through says stay connected through a sag; anti-islanding says trip promptly when the grid is gone. Reconciling them is why modern requirements are detailed curves rather than single thresholds.
  • Which requirement applies depends on when and where. Interconnection standards have been revised repeatedly, and jurisdictions adopt them on their own schedules. An existing DER may be operating to a superseded requirement entirely legitimately.
  • Voltage and frequency ride-through curves define regions of mandatory operation, permissive operation, and mandatory trip, as a function of magnitude and duration.
  • Grid-support functions — volt/VAr, volt/watt, frequency-watt — are increasingly required, which means a DER is now participating in voltage regulation on the same feeder as your regulators and capacitor banks. Coordinate them.
  • Get the settings from the interconnection agreement, not from a default profile. They are contractual, and they are auditable.

Shape is illustrative, not a settings source. The point is that a single voltage threshold cannot satisfy both requirements, which is why the modern requirement is a curve. (Animated in the browser.)

6What the utility side has to change

  • Re-run the coordination study with the DER modeled, in every credible switching configuration and at both maximum and minimum DER output.
  • Add directionality where flow can reverse — a non-directional 51 on a bidirectional circuit is a coordination hazard.
  • Review reclosing. Options include supervising the reclose with sync check, adding a voltage check for a dead line, lengthening the open interval so the DER's anti-islanding has time to act, or transfer-tripping the DER before reclosing.
  • Review fusing. Fuse-saving schemes assume the fuse sees a specific current; DER infeed changes it.
  • Check the voltage profile. DER can raise voltage at its point of connection above limits, and can confuse regulator line-drop compensation, which assumes load flows away from the regulator — see Voltage Regulator Control.
  • Check the grounding of the interconnection transformer. This is the decision that determines whether an unintentional island remains effectively grounded, and it drives arrester ratings on the utility side.
  • Tell the restoration scheme about it. A FLISR scheme that reconfigures the feeder must know where DER is and what it will do — see how the DAC decides.
  • Get visibility. DER output, status, and connection state as SCADA points, so operators are not reasoning about a feeder whose generation they cannot see.

7Gotchas

  • Settings taken from a manufacturer default profile rather than from the interconnection agreement. The agreement is the requirement.
  • Anti-islanding tested only as a settings check. Prove the function by actually separating the source during commissioning, under controlled conditions.
  • Transfer trip channel unmonitored. A dependable method that has silently failed is not dependable. Alarm the channel — and know what the DER does when it is lost.
  • Reclosing unchanged after DER is added. The most consequential omission on this page.
  • Assuming inverters contribute meaningful fault current. Sensitivity calculations that count on it will be wrong.
  • Ignoring aggregate effect. One rooftop system is negligible; four hundred on one feeder is a generator. Track cumulative penetration, not individual applications.
  • DER invisible to operations. A feeder that will not stay de-energized, with no indication why, is an incident waiting to happen.
  • Grounding decided by the customer's transformer vendor. Specify it, in writing, as an interconnection requirement.
