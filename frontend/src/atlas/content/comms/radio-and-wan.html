☰The rules, stated plainly

Links you do not own end to end

  • Bandwidth is rarely the constraint; latency and reliability are. SCADA polling needs very little data and a great deal of predictability.
  • Point-to-point is for speed; point-to-multipoint is for reach. One radio family often does both — the SEL-3031 supports point-to-point for teleprotection and economical backup protection, and point-to-multipoint for SCADA data gathering from many remotes.
  • Line of sight is not enough — the Fresnel zone must be clear too. A path that looks visually clear can still lose most of its signal to an obstruction near the middle.
  • Every link has a budget: transmit power, antenna gains, cable losses, and path loss must leave enough margin above receiver sensitivity to survive rain, foliage growth, and equipment ageing.
  • The protocol must be told the truth about the channel. Timeouts, retries, and inter-character gaps tuned for a fiber link will misbehave over a radio with tens of milliseconds of latency.
  • If it leaves your property, encrypt it and account for it. Unlicensed spectrum and public carriers are shared media by definition.

2Spread-spectrum radio in practice

Frequency-hopping spread spectrum in the license-free 900 MHz ISM band is the workhorse of distribution automation in North America: no licensing delay, adequate range, and enough resistance to narrowband interference to be usable in a shared band.

Point to point

  • Two radios, one path, all the airtime. Lowest and most predictable latency.
  • Fast enough for relay-to-relay signalling: the SEL-3031 quotes a typical 4.8 ms latency for control commands carried over Mirrored Bits.
  • Used for teleprotection, distributed generation interties, and economical backup protection channels where fiber is not available.

Point to multipoint

  • One master radio serving many remotes, sharing airtime. Higher and more variable latency by nature.
  • The standard arrangement for SCADA polling of reclosers, regulators, capacitor controls, and sensors across a service territory.
  • Scaling is about airtime, not bandwidth: doubling the number of remotes roughly doubles the time to poll them all.

Features that matter in the field

  • Multiple independent ports in one radio. The SEL-3031 communicates on up to three independent ports and protocols simultaneously — so one radio can carry SCADA DNP3, a Mirrored Bits channel, and engineering access without three separate links.
  • Protocol transparency. Byte-oriented protocols pass through: DNP3, Modbus, Mirrored Bits, IEEE C37.118 synchrophasors, SEL Fast Messaging, and SEL ASCII.
  • Encryption. An optional encryption card provides session authentication and 256-bit AES. On unlicensed spectrum this is not optional in any meaningful sense — anyone with a compatible radio can listen.
  • Serial port flexibility. EIA-232, EIA-485, or optical fiber for the connection to the device, matching whatever the recloser control or relay presents.
  • A separate management port. A USB management port allows settings changes and performance verification without disturbing the data channels — which matters when the data channels are carrying SCADA for a live feeder.
  • Settings in the usual tool. Radio configuration through ACSELERATOR QuickSet keeps it in the same workflow as the relays.

This is why teleprotection uses a dedicated point-to-point link even on a site that already has a SCADA radio. (Animated in the browser.)

3Path engineering

Fresnel-zone radius grows with distance and falls with frequency; at 900 MHz over a few miles it is tens of feet at midpath. Vendor documentation tabulates it — use the table rather than intuition. (Animated in the browser.)

Survey the path, do not assume it. A desk study using terrain data gets you a candidate; a field test with the actual radios and antennas at the actual mounting heights gets you a link. Record the received signal level at commissioning so you have a baseline to compare against when it degrades three years later.

4Cellular and public carriers

  • Addressing is the first problem. Carrier networks usually place devices behind NAT with dynamic addresses. Options are a private APN with static addressing, a VPN back to the utility network, or an outbound-only architecture where the device initiates the connection.
  • Latency is variable and not yours to control. Set protocol timeouts generously and expect occasional multi-second excursions. Unsolicited reporting suits cellular better than aggressive polling.
  • Data volume costs money. A chatty integrity poll every few seconds across a thousand devices adds up. Tune deadbands and use event-driven reporting — see DNP3 Fundamentals.
  • Coverage is not uniform and can change when a carrier re-farms spectrum or retires a technology. Track end-of-life announcements for the radio modules in your fleet.
  • Security is entirely on you. The carrier network is not a trusted network. Encrypt end to end, authenticate, and terminate into a controlled boundary — see Substation Cybersecurity.

Diagnose the layer, not the symptom. “DNP3 keeps going offline” on a radio link is almost never a DNP3 problem. Check received signal level and error counters at the radio first, then frame-level statistics, then the protocol. Fixing a timeout to paper over a marginal path buys a few months.

6Monitoring the link

  • Received signal level as an analog point, trended. Seasonal variation is normal; a downward trend is a failing connector or growing vegetation.
  • Error and retry counters from the radio and from the protocol master. Rising retries precede outright failure by months.
  • Link up/down status per remote, distinct from the protocol session state — they fail for different reasons.
  • Latency, where the equipment reports it. A step change means a path change.
  • Baseline everything at commissioning and record it in the project file, so “is this normal?” has an answer.

7Gotchas

  • Commissioning on a clear winter day. Foliage in summer can cost several dB on a treed path. Build the margin in.
  • Antenna aimed by eye. A high-gain directional antenna has a narrow beam; a few degrees off costs real signal. Aim it, then verify with the radio's own signal reading.
  • Long or wrong coax. Cable loss at 900 MHz is substantial. Every extra metre is signal you paid for and threw away.
  • Unlicensed band interference appearing later. Somebody else's new system is entirely legal. Frequency hopping helps; margin helps more.
  • No lightning protection. The antenna, the mast, the coax, and the bonding are one system. Get it wrong and you replace radios after every storm — and possibly the equipment they connect to.
  • Protection over a shared, variable-latency link. Backup protection over radio is a legitimate application when the radio is engineered for it; opportunistic use of the SCADA link is not.
  • Encryption not enabled because it was “a pilot.” Pilots become permanent.
  • One radio carrying everything with no fallback. When it fails you lose SCADA, engineering access, and any protection signalling at once. Decide deliberately whether that is acceptable.
