☰The rules, stated plainly

Fiber

  • Match SFPs end to end: mode (MM/SM), wavelength, and speed must agree at both ends of the fiber — and the distance must fit the transceiver's power budget.
  • Fiber leaves the building; copper does not. Ground potential rise during a fault puts the station's whole potential across any copper pair running to another structure. Glass carries no current.
  • Received power must land between the receiver's minimum and maximum. Too little light and the link flaps; a long-haul optic on a short jumper overdrives the far end.
  • Fixed-speed optics do not negotiate. A 100BASE-FX transceiver will never link to a 1000BASE port, no matter how correct everything else is.
  • Most fiber faults are at the ends. Dirty or damaged connectors, not broken cable. Clean before you diagnose.
  • Devices notice transceivers. Relays raise port alarms for non-compliant SFPs, switches log every insertion and removal, and swapping one live can blip communications. Treat it as maintenance.

1Copper, and where it stops

Copper twisted pair — 10/100/1000BASE-T, using shielded cables in SEL practice — is fine inside a panel or a room, with the standard's reach around 100 m. Substation practice puts fiber on anything between buildings or out into the yard, and the reason is not bandwidth.

Ground potential rise A fault current into the ground grid raises the station's potential above remote earth, potentially by thousands of volts. A copper pair to another structure sees that difference across it. See Substation Anatomy.
Electromagnetic interference Switching transients and fault current couple into copper runs. Fiber is immune by construction — no conductor, no induced voltage.
Ground loops Two grounded ends of a copper run form a loop that carries power-frequency current. Fiber breaks the loop completely.
Distance 100 m on copper; kilometres to tens of kilometres on fiber, with no repeaters.
Lightning Copper conducts a surge from a struck structure into your equipment. Glass does not.
What copper is still for Inside the panel, inside one building, and short runs where none of the above applies. Shielded, and grounded at one end — see Control Wiring.

2Multimode and single-mode

The two families never mix on one link. The difference is the diameter of the light-carrying core, and everything else follows from it.

Relay and switch fiber ports use LC connectors on duplex cables in current SEL practice; older equipment uses ST. (Animated in the browser.)

3SFPs — the modular way in

Rather than fixing the optics in the port, most modern equipment takes SFP transceivers ordered separately and chosen by reach. The SEL-2730M's ports 1–4, the SEL-2740S, and the 400-series five-port Ethernet card all work this way. The 400-series compatibility table, condensed:

  • Copper SFPs exist for the occasional copper trunk — supported on the SEL-2730M and SEL-2740S per their release notes.
  • Order the transceivers with the switch. They are separate line items and they are the thing most often forgotten, discovered on the day of installation.
  • Keep spares matching what is installed, and keep them labeled. An unlabeled SFP in a drawer is a mystery.

4The link budget

The transceiver table's TX power and RX sensitivity columns are the link budget. Everything between them has to fit.

Measure received power at commissioning with an optical power meter and record it. It is the baseline that makes a future degradation obvious. (Animated in the browser.)

  • Too little light — distance, extra splices, dirty connectors, a bend beyond the fiber's radius — and the link flaps or never comes up.
  • Too much light — a long-haul optic on a short jumper — overdrives the far receiver. Attenuators exist for exactly this.
  • Bend radius is real. A cable tie pulled tight or a cable folded into a tray corner produces loss that will not be found by looking at the ends.

5Handling and cleaning

  • Clean before you connect, every time. A single particle on a core face is enough. Use a proper cleaner — a click-type cleaning tool or lint-free wipes with the correct solvent — not a shirt sleeve.
  • Caps on when disconnected, on both the connector and the port. Dust is the enemy and it is everywhere in a substation.
  • Do not look into a fiber or a transmitting port. The light is invisible and it is focused.
  • Respect minimum bend radius, in the tray, in the panel, and inside the splice enclosure — which is where it is most often violated.
  • Label both ends of every strand, with the same identifier. Tracing unlabeled fiber in a splice enclosure is a genuinely miserable job.
  • Keep spare strands. Pull more fiber than you need; the incremental cost is small and the future cost of not having it is large.

6Troubleshooting & fine print

  • Link up in one direction only → TX/RX strands swapped at one end of a duplex LC pair. Swap them at one end, not both.
  • No link at all on new fiber → mode or wavelength mismatch, speed mismatch, or received power outside the transceiver's budget. Check the SFP table before blaming the cable.
  • Link that comes up and drops → marginal optical budget. Measure received power; look for a dirty connector or a stressed bend.
  • Works cold, fails in summer → a marginal budget plus temperature, or a splice enclosure taking sun.
  • “SFP not compliant” port alarm → a wrong or third-party transceiver for that card and firmware. SEL switches log every SFP insertion and removal as a security event, so the history is in the log.
  • Communications blipped while somebody was in the cabinet → the RTAC manual warns that swapping SFP ports live can interrupt communications. Treat transceiver work as a planned maintenance activity.
  • Intermittent in the yard → almost always a connector at a splice enclosure, not a broken cable. Clean and re-seat before excavating anything.
