The arithmetic you will use every day

  • Everything is RMS unless it says otherwise. A “120 V” PT secondary peaks at 170 V. Relay settings, nameplates, and meters are RMS; oscillography and insulation ratings are peak.
  • A phasor is a snapshot of a spinning vector. Magnitude plus angle, all referenced to one arbitrary zero — almost always VA. Angles only mean something relative to that reference.
  • Wye: VLL = √3 × VLN, Iline = Iphase. Delta: VLL = Vphase, Iline = √3 × Iphase. Getting this backwards is the single most common wiring-and-settings error.
  • S = √3 · VLL · Iline for a balanced three-phase circuit, in VA. P = S cos φ, Q = S sin φ.
  • Faults are inductive; load is not. Load current sits near unity power factor; fault current lags by 60–85°. That angle difference is what every directional element lives on.
  • Per-unit divides out the transformers. Zbase = Vbase² / Sbase. Once everything is in per-unit on one base, you can add impedances across voltage levels without thinking about turns ratios.

1Sine, RMS, and the rotating phasor

A 60 Hz voltage is v(t) = Vpeak sin(ωt + θ), with ω = 2π·60 = 377 rad/s. Working with that time function directly is miserable, so the whole industry does one trick: freeze the rotation. Draw the peak value as an arrow at angle θ, agree that everything rotates counter-clockwise at the same speed, and now addition of waveforms becomes addition of arrows.

RMS is the value that delivers the same heating as an equal DC value. For a sine wave, Vrms = Vpeak / √2 = 0.707 Vpeak. Phasor magnitudes in protection work are RMS by convention, so a 120 V phasor means 120 V RMS and 169.7 V peak.

The arrow and the wave are the same object. Protection engineers work with the arrow because arrows add; relays compute the arrow from samples of the wave using a Fourier filter. (Animated in the browser.)

What the relay actually does. A digital relay samples each input (typically 16–32 samples/cycle), then runs a one-cycle discrete Fourier transform to extract the fundamental phasor — magnitude and angle at exactly 60 Hz — discarding harmonics and DC offset. That is why relay metering reads the fundamental while a true-RMS clamp meter reads everything: on a distorted waveform the two legitimately disagree. See Event Reports & Oscillography for filtered versus raw.

2Three phase, and the two connections

Three sources 120° apart deliver constant instantaneous power, need no neutral when balanced, and let a motor start itself. Everything downstream inherits that structure. The two ways to connect three windings — wye and delta — produce different relationships between what you measure at a terminal and what exists in a winding.

√3 is not a magic constant; it is what you get subtracting two equal phasors 120° apart. (The dashed construction line animates in the browser.)

The PT connection changes what the relay can protect. Wye-connected PTs give the relay VA, VB, VC and therefore 3V0 — needed for ground directional elements and for 59N. Open-delta PTs give only line-to-line voltages: no zero sequence, no ground directional. Check which one you have before promising a ground directional element. See CT & PT Fundamentals.

3Real, reactive, and apparent power

Current that is in phase with voltage does work. Current that is 90° out of phase does not — it sloshes energy into and out of magnetic and electric fields twice per cycle. Split the current into those two components and you get the power triangle.

Reactive power is not wasted energy — it is energy that never leaves. But the current carrying it is real, and it heats real conductors. (Animated in the browser.)

Sign conventions, the perennial argument. By IEEE convention, power flowing out of the bus into the line is positive, a lagging (inductive) load absorbs positive Q, and a capacitor supplies negative Q (i.e. absorbs −Q). Relays let you flip the reference with a CT polarity setting, which means half the “wrong sign” calls are actually a polarity setting, not a wiring error. Four-quadrant metering is covered in Revenue & Panel Metering.

4Impedance and the X/R ratio

Z = R + jX. Resistance dissipates; reactance stores. What matters in protection is the ratio, because it decides both the fault current angle and how much DC offset the fault carries.

ElementTypical X/RFault current lags byConsequence

DC offset is not a measurement error. When a fault strikes at a voltage zero on a highly inductive circuit, the current must start at zero, so it carries an exponentially decaying DC component that can nearly double the first peak. Time constant τ = X / (ωR) — so a high X/R means the offset lingers for many cycles. This is what saturates CTs, what makes the first cycle of an event report look asymmetric, and why relays use filters that reject DC. See CT & PT Fundamentals and Faults & Fault Current.

5Per-unit — the unit system that deletes transformers

Pick a base power (Sbase, one value for the whole study) and a base voltage for each voltage level (Vbase, normally nominal line-to-line). Everything else follows:

Base quantityFormulaExample: 100 MVA base, 12.47 kV
Base currentIbase = Sbase / (√3 · Vbase)100 MVA / (1.732 × 12.47 kV) = 4630 A
Base impedanceZbase = Vbase² / Sbase(12.47 kV)² / 100 MVA = 1.555 Ω
Per-unit valueXpu = Xactual / Zbase0.5 Ω line = 0.32 pu
RebasingZnew = Zold · (Snew/Sold) · (Vold/Vnew)²a 10% transformer on its own 20 MVA base = 0.50 pu on 100 MVA

The payoff: a per-unit impedance is the same number on both sides of a transformer. A short-circuit study can add the source, transformer, and line impedances in a single series string with no turns-ratio arithmetic, and fault current in per-unit is simply 1 / Ztotal.

Worked example. A 12.47 kV bus is fed by a 20 MVA transformer with 8% impedance from a stiff source. On a 100 MVA base the transformer is 0.08 × (100/20) = 0.40 pu. Ignoring source impedance, three-phase fault current = 1 / 0.40 = 2.5 pu = 2.5 × 4630 = 11,600 A. That single number sets your instantaneous pickup, your CT requirements, and your equipment duty.

Per-unit inside the relay

Relays use a second, smaller per-unit system: quantities normalized to the relay's nominal inputs. A “1.0 pu voltage” setting means nominal secondary voltage (typically 67 V line-to-neutral or 120 V line-to-line); a per-unit current setting is referenced to the nominal CT secondary (5 A or 1 A). This is why the same setting file will not simply drop into a relay with a different nominal input rating — see Settings Management.

6Phase rotation and reference

Rotation (or phase sequence) is the order in which the phases reach their peaks: ABC (positive/“normal”) or ACB. It is a property of the system, and it must be set correctly in the relay — on SEL relays the PHROT setting — because every negative-sequence and directional calculation depends on it.

  • Get it wrong and positive and negative sequence swap places. A healthy balanced system will then appear to the relay as pure negative sequence: 46 elements pick up, directional elements point the wrong way, and nothing about the metering looks obviously broken.
  • Angles are relative. Relay displays reference everything to VA (or to the first available voltage). An event report showing IA at −75° means seventy-five degrees lagging that reference, not any absolute truth.
  • Prove rotation on site with a rotation meter or by reading the relay's own sequence quantities under known load. Do not infer it from wire colors.

7Gotchas

  • √3 applied twice, or not at all. The classic scaling bug: a value that is 1.73× or 0.577× what it should be is almost never a CT problem, it is a wye/delta assumption.
  • RMS versus fundamental. On a feeder with heavy harmonics, a true-RMS clamp meter and the relay's metering will differ by several percent. Both are right; they measure different things.
  • “120 V” is ambiguous. It may mean 120 V line-to-line (so 69.3 V line-to-neutral) or 120 V line-to-neutral (208 V line-to-line). Always ask which, and check the relay's VNOM/PTR settings against the actual PT.
  • kVA is not kW. Transformer and conductor ratings are apparent power. Sizing on kW at an assumed unity power factor undersizes real installations.
  • Per-unit values are meaningless without their base. “8% impedance” on the nameplate means 8% on the transformer's own rating. Rebase before combining.
  • Angle sign conventions vary. Some tools show current angle leading positive, some lagging positive. Confirm with a known case (load at near unity) before trusting a directional conclusion.
