☰The rules, stated plainly

  • A relay never sees primary quantities — only CT/PT secondaries. The CTR/PTR settings are the only bridge back; get them wrong and every metered value, pickup, and event report is wrong by the same factor.
  • Never open an energized CT secondary. Short it first. SEL-751/751A warning (identical text in both manuals): "Before working on a CT circuit, first apply a short to the secondary winding of the CT."
  • A C-rating is a voltage: C400 delivers 400 V at 20 × rated secondary current into a standard burden with ≤10 % ratio error. On a reduced tap, the rating de-rates proportionally.
  • Saturation is driven by dc offset (X/R), remanence, and burden — not just fault magnitude. A fully offset fault can saturate a CT that handles the same symmetrical current fine.
  • LOP is not a fault: voltage drops with no change in current → block voltage-dependent elements, don't trip.

1Why the relay never sees the primary

Current transformers (CTs) and voltage/potential transformers (VTs/PTs) scale kiloamps and kilovolts down to bench-safe secondary quantities — nominally 5 A or 1 A per phase and roughly 67 V L-N / 120 V L-L class voltages — and isolate the relay from the primary system. Everything the relay measures, meters, and trips on is a secondary quantity; the ratio settings tell it how to report primary units:

2CT basics: ratio, polarity, 1 A vs 5 A

  • Ratio. Nameplate primary : secondary — a 600/5 CT has a turns ratio of 120. Multi-ratio CTs are tapped; use the ratio of the tap actually wired (and remember the C-rating de-rates with it, §3).
  • Polarity. Instrument transformers carry polarity marks (the dot / H1-X1 convention): current into the marked primary terminal flows out of the marked secondary terminal. On SEL rear panels the convention is explicit — on the SEL-451 the odd-numbered terminals (Z01, Z03 … / Z13, Z15 …) are the polarity terminals for CT and PT inputs. Directional, power, and differential measurements are only as correct as these connections; commissioning checks (§8) verify angle as well as magnitude.
  • 1 A vs 5 A nominal. Ordered per model (INOM); same relay algorithms, different input scaling. The SEL-751A also offers 50 mA and 2.5 mA high-sensitivity inputs for core-balance CTs; the SEL-751's sensitive-ground equivalent is a 200 mA-INOM neutral channel (ungrounded, high-impedance, and Petersen-coil-grounded systems), plus optional LEA Rogowski-coil/LPCT low-energy inputs.

The safety rule: a live CT secondary must always see a closed loop. Both manuals repeat it — short the secondary winding before working on any CT circuit, and make sure secondaries are shorted before they are disconnected from the relay. The reason falls out of the CT model (§5): whatever current the secondary loop doesn't carry becomes magnetizing current. Open the loop and the entire ratio current is forced into the magnetizing branch, driving the core far past saturation — the winding then develops dangerous peak voltages at the open terminals. SEL Connectorized relays build the rule into hardware: the CT connector internally shorts each CT as you unplug it.

3Accuracy class: what C400 actually promises

ANSI/IEEE Class C CTs (the common protection class in North America, negligible leakage flux) are rated by a secondary terminal voltage: the CT will deliver that voltage into a standard burden at 20 × rated secondary current without exceeding 10 % ratio error. For a 5 A CT, C400 therefore means 400 V at 100 A secondary — which is a burden statement in disguise: 400 V ÷ 100 A = 4 Ω of total burden at the rating limit.

  • Tap de-rating: a C400, 2000/5 MR CT wired on its 1200/5 tap is only good for (1200/2000) × 400 = 240 V.
  • The excitation curve is the real datasheet. Secondary excitation voltage vs. excitation (magnetizing) current, log-log. The knee is where maximum core permeability occurs (typically μR ≈ 5000–15000); past it, permeability collapses toward 1 and a small rise in voltage demands a huge rise in magnetizing current. In SEL's worked example (C400, 2000/5, RCT = 0.7 Ω), the excitation voltage at 10 A of excitation current — the 10 %-error point at 100 A — is 496 V: the manufacturer curve buys you margin over the conservative "use the C number" assumption.
  • Every volt is generated across the magnetizing branch. The CT must magnetize its core to push secondary current through the total burden; the voltage it needs is Isec × (RCT + Rleads + Zrelay). Keep that below the knee and ratio error stays negligible.

4Burden: the load decides whether the CT tells the truth

Burden is everything the secondary current must be pushed through: the CT's own winding resistance, the lead run to the panel, and the relay input. Numeric relays changed this game — an SEL-451 presents ~0.27 VA at 5 A (≈ 0.01 Ω) where electromechanical relays presented ohms — so on modern installations lead resistance usually dominates. Two field rules from the SEL CT model documentation:

  • For single-line-to-ground faults with wye-connected CTs, double the lead resistance — the current returns through the neutral lead, so the voltage drop is out-and-back.
  • Enter burden as the complex sum of lead resistance and relay (restraint) burden when simulating; for digital relays the operate-circuit impedance is effectively 0.

The excitation curve as a burden meter: raising total burden (or fault current) raises the voltage the CT must generate. Below the knee the magnetizing current — the error — is milliamps; past the knee it takes off, and current that should reach the relay magnetizes the core instead. ▶ Animated in the HTML edition.

5Saturation: flux walks up until the CT quits

A fault's dc offset is what usually kills the CT, not its symmetrical magnitude. The offset is set by the system X/R ratio and the fault incidence angle: current is IPS[sin(ωt + θ − φ) − sin(θ − φ)e−t/τ] with time constant τ = X/(2πfR). θ = 0 gives full offset; θ = atan(2πfL/R) gives none. The dc component is one-signed, so the core flux — the integral of the voltage the CT must generate — ratchets upward every cycle instead of averaging out. When flux reaches the saturation level, magnetizing inductance collapses (μR → 1), the low-impedance magnetizing branch swallows the current, and the secondary output drops out.

Bottom: the delivered secondary current — faithful for the first cycle and a half, then collapsing to spikes each time the core re-saturates, recovering slowly as the dc decays. ▶ Animated in the HTML edition.

  • Remanence — residual flux left in the core from a previous event — biases the CT toward saturation and shortens time-to-saturate; SEL's simulation tool treats up to 80 % remanence as the worst case and warns not to exceed it.
  • Time-to-saturate is what your instantaneous elements are racing. In the SEL tool you read it directly off the secondary-current plot as the sharp drop in output; maximize offset (θ = 0) and add remanence for the worst case.
  • Generator zones hurt most — X/R approaching 20 or more means a long-lived dc offset, and space limits often prevent choosing CTs big enough to avoid saturation entirely.
  • The relay's A/D clips too, independent of the CT: SEL-700 series limits at 217 A peak (5 A ch) / 43 A pk (1 A ch); SEL-400 series 247.5 / 49.5 A pk; SEL-300 series 226 / 45 A pk.

6What saturation does to protection — and how relays fight back

  • Overcurrent elements underreach and slow down. The filtered fundamental of a saturated waveform reads far below the true current — SEL's classic example: a 40 kA fault on C50, 100:5 CTs where the fundamental magnitude never reaches the trip threshold and the relay does not operate. Elements become "slow or inoperative."
  • Differential security is jeopardized on through faults. Unequal saturation of the two CTs manufactures false operate current. Low-impedance differentials answer with slope restraint (restraint = k·(|IL| + |IR|); k = 1 for SEL-400/700 series, 0.5 for SEL-300/500 series) — and the secure slope for heavy through faults is hard to calculate by hand, which is exactly what the AG2019-20 simulation tool is for. 7The PT/VT side: connections, fusing, LOP

Voltage inputs are the easy half — no saturation race, just connection topology and supervision. The SEL-751A accepts (Figures 2.18/2.19; same menu on the SEL-751, Figures 2.22/2.23): direct connection on a grounded system, wye-wye VTs (four-wire, phase-to-neutral → DELTA_Y := WYE), open-delta VTs — two transformers measuring A-B and C-B, the standard two-VT economy connection (→ DELTA_Y := DELTA) — or a single VT (SINGLEV := Y; the relay then assumes balanced voltages for power and sequence quantities — 751A: A input only, A-N or A-B; 751: any input via SING_VIN). The SEL-451, by contrast, takes only three-phase four-wire wye potentials (67 V L-N nominal, 300 V continuous, 600 V for 10 s, burden 0.03 VA @ 67 V).

  • VNOM is always line-to-line, even in wye. For VNOM above 250 V (L-L) use a solidly grounded wye system, or a line-to-ground fault raises the healthy-phase terminal voltages by 1.73×.
  • Fuse the VT secondaries (F1–F3 in every connection diagram); SEL recommends current-limiting fuses in direct-connected voltage applications to limit arc-flash incident energy.

Loss-of-potential (60LOP) — the fuse-blown detector

Blown VT fuses or a tripped molded-case breaker present the relay with a voltage collapse that looks like a fault — except the currents don't move. The SEL-751A declares LOP on a >25 % drop in positive-sequence voltage V1 (the SEL-751 uses >20 % — same logic, tighter threshold) with no corresponding change in I1, I2, or I0; after 1 second it latches the LOP Relay Word bit. Always active unless blocked via LOPBLK (default 0). Use LOP in SELOGIC to supervise everything that trusts voltage — undervoltage 27 and directional power elements especially — so a dropped wrench on the fuse holders doesn't become a trip. Full logic and element supervision: see Protection Elements in atlas.

8Field practice: shorting, grounding, proving the wiring

  • Short before you touch. Apply a short to the CT secondary before working the circuit, and confirm secondaries are shorted before disconnecting them from the relay. Use the shorting hardware you have: SEL Connectorized terminals internally short each CT as the connector is withdrawn — pull it straight off the rear panel.
  • Ground the secondary circuit at one point: the manuals' connection diagrams put it plainly — the CTs and the relay chassis should be grounded in the relay cabinet.
  • Check the nameplate before energizing: relay misoperation can result from applying other than the specified secondary voltages and currents — verify nominal voltage and current on the rear-panel label against the instrument transformers.

Angle conventions matter: for wye (DELTA_Y := WYE, ABC rotation) inject VA/IA at 0°, VB/IB at −120°, VC/IC at +120°; for open-delta inject VAB at 30° and VCB at 90° (ABC). If a device is reachable but meters wrong by a clean factor, it's the ratio settings; if angles are rotated or a power reading is negative, suspect rolled phases or a polarity-reversed CT lead.
