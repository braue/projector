☰The rules, stated plainly

Wiring practice

  • CT circuits short before they open. Every test switch, shorting terminal block, and procedure exists to guarantee that order. Get it backwards and you have an open CT secondary at lethal voltage.
  • PT circuits open before they short. The opposite rule — a PT is a voltage source and shorting it is a fault. Test switch blades for potential and for current are deliberately different, and they are not interchangeable.
  • One shield ground per run. Ground the shield at one end only (normally the panel end) so power-frequency current cannot circulate through it.
  • Separate AC, DC, and signal. Different ducts or at least different bundles. Coupling from a trip-coil pulse into an unshielded signal pair is a real and repeatable phenomenon.
  • Label as you land. An unlabeled wire is unlabeled forever, because nobody goes back.
  • Torque matters. Loose terminations heat, oxidize, and eventually open — and a loose CT terminal is an open CT waiting for the next fault.

Terminal-block numbering is the vocabulary of field troubleshooting. “TB4-7” means block four, terminal seven, and it should mean the same thing on the drawing, on the block, and in the test procedure. When the three disagree, stop and reconcile before doing anything else.

2Test switches — and the sequence that keeps you alive

An FT-1-style test switch (and its many equivalents) puts a hinged blade in each circuit so a relay can be isolated and a test set inserted without unlanding a wire. The critical property is the order in which contacts make and break as the blade swings.

Make-before-break on current, break-before-make on potential. Confirm which blade you are touching before you touch it. (Animated in the browser.)

Trust the blade, but verify. Test switches age; contacts corrode and shorting bars wear. Before opening a current blade on unfamiliar equipment, confirm the short with a clamp meter on the shorted path, or install an external shorting jumper on the terminal block first. It costs two minutes.

Separation and shielding

  • Route power, control, and signal cables in separate trays or ducts where possible, and cross at right angles where they must cross.
  • Twist plus shield beats shield alone. Twisting rejects magnetically induced noise; the shield handles capacitive coupling. Both matter in a substation.
  • Ground the shield at one end. Grounding both ends creates a loop that carries power-frequency and fault current, injecting exactly the noise the shield was supposed to stop. The usual exception is high-frequency-dominated environments, where a capacitive ground at the far end is used — follow the site standard, and be consistent.
  • Keep the shield continuous and land it on the panel ground bar, not on a random screw. Pigtails should be short.
  • Anything leaving the control house on copper deserves surge protection and, ideally, should not be copper at all. See Substation Anatomy on ground potential rise.

4Relay I/O: what the terminals actually expect

Binary inputs

  • Optically isolated inputs with a voltage threshold. Ordering options set the range (24, 48, 125, or 250 V DC families), and a 125 V input will not reliably operate on 48 V.
  • Wetting voltage comes from the station battery through the field contact. The relay input is the load; the contact is the switch.
  • Long cable runs have capacitance. A high-impedance input plus a long cable can hold enough charge to look asserted after the contact opens — this is why relays offer a settable input debounce and, on some models, a higher pickup threshold option.
  • SEL relays let you assign each input in SELogic and set a debounce time; use it rather than fighting chatter downstream in the RTAC.

Output contacts

  • Standard versus high-current interrupting. Trip-rated outputs can make and carry trip-coil current; general-purpose contacts often cannot interrupt inductive DC. Check the ordering option against the duty.
  • Form A / Form B / Form C — normally open, normally closed, changeover. Choose by fail-safe behavior: what should this contact do when the relay loses power?
  • Interposing relays bridge the gap when a relay output cannot handle the load, when many contacts are needed, or when circuit separation is required. They add 5–15 ms — acceptable for alarms, sometimes not for protection.
  • Minimum trip duration (SEL TDURD-style settings) holds the output asserted long enough for the breaker to act; the 52a contact does the interrupting.
Decide the de-energized state deliberately. A trip output should be normally open (a dead relay must not trip). An alarm contact is often normally closed, so loss of the relay itself raises the alarm. Choosing by habit rather than by intent is how a failed device silently stops reporting.

5Landing wires well

  • Strip to the right length. Bare conductor visible outside the terminal is a short waiting to happen; insulation inside the clamp is a joint that will heat.
  • Ferrules on stranded conductors where the block calls for them; never tin stranded wire for a screw terminal — solder creeps and the joint loosens.
  • One conductor per terminal unless the block is explicitly designed for two. Doubling up under a screw guarantees an intermittent later.
  • Torque to specification, and re-check after the first thermal cycle on anything carrying real current.
  • Dress the bundle with a service loop at each device so a relay can be removed without unlanding half the panel.
  • Label both ends with the wire number from the drawing. Heat-shrink markers survive; adhesive labels in a hot panel do not.
  • Leave spare capacity. Spare terminals, spare pairs in every cable, and duct fill under the limit. Every one of them will be used within five years.

7Gotchas

  • The shorting screw left in. The single most common commissioning error, and the most consequential: the relay sees no current and protects nothing. Count them in, count them out.
  • Two shields grounded. Produces intermittent noise that tracks load current and is maddening to chase. Check both ends of any suspect run.
  • Wetting voltage borrowed from the wrong supply. Inputs wetted from a different DC source than the contact's circuit create a path between supplies — and a DC ground on one becomes a DC ground on both.
  • Interposing relay contacts rated for AC only. They will weld on DC. Check the DC rating specifically.
  • Spare wires left unterminated and unlabeled in a live panel. Cap and land them on spare terminals, and mark them on the drawing.
  • Terminal blocks used as splices. Acceptable and normal — but only if the drawing shows it. An undocumented jumper is a future misdiagnosis.
  • Megger applied with a device connected. Instant, expensive, and entirely avoidable. Build it into the written procedure, not into someone's memory.
