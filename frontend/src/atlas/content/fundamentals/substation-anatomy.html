☰The rules, stated plainly

Reading a station

  • The bus arrangement is a reliability-versus-cost decision, and it dictates the protection. How many breakers per circuit, and what an outage of any one of them costs, is the whole design question.
  • Protection zones are bounded by CTs, and adjacent zones must overlap. A gap between zones is a piece of equipment nothing protects.
  • Everything in the control house runs on the battery. Lose station DC and you lose tripping, closing, and most of your intelligence — see Station DC Systems.
  • The ground grid is safety equipment. It exists to keep step and touch potentials survivable during a fault, not primarily to give relays a reference.
  • Nothing in the yard is single-phase. Three of everything, and the middle phase is usually the one you cannot reach.

2Bus arrangements

The layout answers one question: when a breaker must come out of service — for maintenance or because it failed — what else goes with it?

Reliability rises left to right and top to bottom; so does the number of breakers, the number of CT sets, and the amount of protection logic. (Animated in the browser.)

Ring and breaker-and-a-half change how you get line current. The line relay must add the currents from two breakers' CT sets, which means both sets must have the same ratio and correct polarity, and a breaker-failure or an open breaker changes what the sum means. It also means each breaker needs its own breaker-failure scheme — see Breaker Failure.

3Protection zones and overlap

A protection zone is bounded by the CTs that feed the relay protecting it. Every piece of primary equipment must be inside at least one zone, and adjacent zones must overlap — usually by placing CTs on both sides of a breaker — so that nothing falls in a gap.

On ring and breaker-and-a-half arrangements this overlap is the norm rather than the exception, which is one reason those schemes carry more relays. (Animated in the browser.)

Where the automation engineer actually stands. Your working area is the automation cabinet and the relay panel's terminal blocks. Everything you build reaches the yard through those terminal blocks, and everything you report reaches the world through that WAN circuit. Know both ends cold.

5Ground grid, GPR, and why it matters to you

A buried mesh of conductor ties every structure, fence, equipment frame, and cable shield to a common reference. During a ground fault, current flowing into that grid raises the whole station's potential above remote earth — ground potential rise — which can be thousands of volts.

  • Step and touch potential are the differences a person can bridge, and grid design targets keeping them survivable. This is why the grid is a safety system first.
  • GPR attacks your communications. A copper pair leaving the station toward remote earth sees the full GPR across it. This is the reason substation communications go out on fiber, and why any copper that must leave gets isolation transformers or protectors. See Fiber & SFPs.
  • Single-point shield grounding inside the station keeps power-frequency current out of signal shields; the practice and its exceptions are in Control Wiring & Terminations.
  • Never assume two “grounds” are the same potential during a fault. They are bonded, which is not the same as equal.

7Gotchas

  • The as-drawn bus arrangement may have been extended. Stations grow. A “single bus” station with a bus tie added in 1998 behaves like a sectionalized one, and its protection may or may not have been revisited.
  • Disconnect switches are not load-break devices. Operating one under load produces a spectacular and dangerous arc. Interlocking that prevents it is protection-grade logic, not a convenience.
  • CT location relative to the breaker decides the zone. Bushing CTs on the line side versus the bus side of the same breaker move the zone boundary — check the three-line, not the one-line.
  • Station service failure is slow-motion trouble. The battery carries the station for hours, then everything stops. Alarm loss of AC and loss of charger separately.
  • Yard fiber is fragile at the ends. Most “intermittent comms” in a yard is a dirty or stressed connector at a splice enclosure, not a broken cable.
  • Anything in the yard may be energized from a source you did not consider — backfeed from a DER, from a station service transformer, or from an adjacent circuit through a closed tie. Confirm before touching.
