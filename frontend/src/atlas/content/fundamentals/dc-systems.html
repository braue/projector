☰The rules, stated plainly

Station DC

  • The battery is the only thing that works when everything else has failed. That is its entire job: hold up protection, control, and communications through a station AC outage.
  • The charger runs the station; the battery covers the gap. In normal operation the charger supplies the load and floats the battery. Losing the charger is not immediately visible — and that is the danger.
  • DC is ungrounded, and one ground does nothing. The second ground completes a circuit that can trip a breaker, block a trip, or short the battery. Find and clear the first one.
  • Selectivity on DC is real work. A fault on one circuit must clear its own fuse or breaker without dropping the whole panel — and DC arcs do not self-extinguish at a current zero, because there is none.
  • Trip coils are short-time rated. The 52a contact, not the relay, is what interrupts trip-coil current — see One-Lines & Schematics.
  • Battery condition is a maintenance program, not an alarm. A battery that has never been capacity-tested is a battery of unknown capability.

1Why the station runs on DC

  • It survives the AC outage. A fault that drops station service must not also disable the protection that is trying to clear it.
  • It is a stored-energy source with predictable behavior. Trip coils and closing mechanisms need a reliable current pulse at a known voltage, on demand, with no dependence on generation.
  • No zero crossings, no phase. Simple, deterministic contact logic; no synchronism concerns in control circuits.

2The three pieces and what each does

In normal operation the battery does nothing but sit at float. That is why a dead battery can go unnoticed for years unless someone tests it. (Animated in the browser.)

Sizing follows a duty cycle: a defined sequence of loads over a defined time (commonly 8 hours), including the momentary high-current pulses of tripping and closing at the end, when the battery is at its weakest. The end-of-discharge voltage limit is what determines whether the last trip works.

The charger

  • Float — the normal mode: a constant voltage that holds the battery full and carries the station load. Roughly 2.17–2.25 V per lead-acid cell, giving about 130–135 V on a 60-cell 125 V system.
  • Equalize — a periodic elevated voltage to bring weak cells back into line on flooded batteries. Higher bus voltage during equalize, which some connected equipment dislikes; VRLA batteries generally must not be equalized.
  • Current limit — the charger will not deliver unlimited current, so after a deep discharge the recharge takes many hours. Plan on that during restoration.
  • Alarms — charger failure, AC input failure, DC overvoltage and undervoltage, and ground detection. All of these deserve to be individual SCADA points.

3DC grounds — the quiet hazard

Station DC is deliberately ungrounded: neither the positive nor the negative rail is bonded to earth. That way a single accidental ground anywhere in the system does not cause current to flow and nothing misoperates. It is a fault-tolerant design — but only for the first fault.

The same mechanism runs in reverse: a pair of grounds in the wrong places can also short out a trip coil, so the breaker refuses to trip when asked. Both failure modes are silent until they matter. (Animated in the browser.)

Finding a DC ground

  1. Detect. A ground detector measures each rail to earth. Balanced (roughly half the bus voltage on each rail) is healthy; one rail near zero and the other near full bus voltage means a ground on the rail reading near zero.
  2. Confirm it is real, not a leakage path through a surge-protective device or a piece of test equipment someone left connected.
  3. Halve the system. Open one DC branch breaker at a time, watching the detector. When the indication clears, the ground is on that branch. Coordinate this with operations — you are removing protection while you do it.
  4. Halve the branch. Continue subdividing at terminal blocks until you reach the offending cable or device.
  5. Expect the usual suspects: a chafed conductor at a cable entry or a hinge, moisture in an outdoor cabinet or breaker mechanism, a failed surge suppressor, or a device with a leaky internal filter capacitor.
Treat a DC ground as an active fault, not a nuisance alarm. The station is now one chafed wire away from an unexplained trip or a blocked trip. Utilities that formally require clearing DC grounds within a set number of hours do so for a good reason.

4Distribution and selectivity

  • Separate circuits for separate functions. Trip circuits, close circuits, relay power, communications, and lighting each get their own protective device — and dual-trip breakers get two entirely independent trip supplies.
  • Selectivity matters more than on AC. A branch fault that trips the main DC breaker takes the whole station's protection out. Verify coordination between the branch device and the main, using DC ratings.
  • Devices must be DC-rated. A DC arc has no current zero to help it extinguish, so an AC-rated breaker or fuse is not a substitute. Voltage rating for DC service is specific and non-negotiable.
  • Cable voltage drop is a design constraint. Trip-coil current pulses on long runs can drop enough voltage that the coil sees less than its minimum pickup at end-of-discharge battery voltage. This is why the sizing calculation puts the trip at the end of the duty cycle.
  • Label everything. Which breaker feeds which panel is exactly the knowledge you will need at 2 a.m. with a ground on the system.

6Maintenance you will encounter

  • Monthly/quarterly inspection — float voltage, connection integrity, electrolyte level and appearance, ambient temperature, corrosion.
  • Annual detailed inspection — individual cell voltages, connection resistance measurements, and specific gravity on flooded batteries.
  • Capacity (load) test — discharging the battery through a known load and timing it against the duty cycle. This is the only test that actually proves capacity; the industry practice (IEEE 450 for vented lead-acid, IEEE 1188 for VRLA) defines intervals and the end-of-life threshold, commonly 80% of rated capacity.
  • Impedance / conductance trending — a proxy for cell health, especially on VRLA where a capacity test is disruptive. Useful for trends, not as a substitute for a capacity test.
A capacity test removes the battery from service. The station is on the charger alone for the duration, so a station AC loss during the test is a real exposure. Plan it, coordinate it, and know how to abort.

7Gotchas

  • Never open a DC circuit under load casually. DC arcs sustain. Use the disconnecting device provided, and be aware that terminal blocks are not switches.
  • Polarity matters and is not always obvious. Reversing a DC supply into a relay or an RTAC can damage the input. Confirm with a meter, not with wire color.
  • The charger's equalize mode raises bus voltage above float for hours. Equipment with a narrow input range, and any DC undervoltage/overvoltage alarm thresholds, must tolerate it.
  • Test equipment can create a DC ground. Grounded-chassis instruments connected to a floating DC system are a common source of “a ground appeared while you were on site.”
  • Battery room ventilation is a real hazard control on flooded batteries — charging generates hydrogen. Do not disable the fan, and do not work with an ignition source in the room.
  • A battery is a very low-impedance source. Dropping a wrench across the terminals is a several-thousand-ampere event. Insulated tools, one hand, no jewelry.
  • Both trip supplies through one breaker defeats the dual-trip design. Verify the actual feed of TC1 and TC2 at the panel, not on the drawing.
