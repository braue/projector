☰The rules, stated plainly

Reading the drawing set

  • Each drawing answers one question. One-line: what is connected to what. Three-line: how the instrument transformers are wired. Elementary: how the control logic works. Connection diagram: which wire lands on which screw.
  • Contacts are drawn de-energized and de-operated. A contact shown open is open with the coil unpowered and the device in its normal (usually closed-breaker-free) state. This one convention explains most drawing confusion.
  • 52a follows the breaker; 52b opposes it. 52a is closed when the breaker is closed. 52b is closed when the breaker is open. Memorize this; you will use it daily.
  • Polarity marks are not decoration. The dot on a CT or PT defines the direction of positive current, and getting one backwards inverts every directional and differential decision.
  • The drawing is a claim, not a fact. Trace and verify before you rely on it. Red-line what you find, and make sure the red-line becomes an as-built.

The one-line is the index. Start there on any unfamiliar station, find the equipment you care about, note its device designation, and then pull the three-line and elementary for that designation. Working the other direction — starting from a schematic without knowing where it sits — wastes hours.

2One-line symbols, and a feeder to read

Everything on a one-line is single-phase shorthand. The moment you need to know how the CTs are actually connected, you have outgrown it and need the three-line. (Animated in the browser.)

Device designations

Equipment is tagged so drawings, settings, and points lists can all refer to the same object. 52-1 is circuit breaker number 1 (52 being the ANSI number for an AC circuit breaker); 86T is the transformer lockout relay; R-14 is recloser 14. The suffix conventions vary between utilities, but the ANSI device number always carries the function — see ANSI Device Numbers.

3The three-line: polarity and where the ground goes

The three-line drawing is where CT ratio, CT connection, polarity, grounding point, and relay input assignment all become explicit. Two details cause most field errors:

  • Polarity. Current entering the polarity-marked primary terminal produces current leaving the polarity-marked secondary terminal. Every directional element, every differential zone, and every power measurement depends on it. A reversed CT typically shows up as power flowing the wrong way on one phase, or as a differential current equal to twice the through current.
  • One ground per circuit. A CT secondary circuit is grounded at exactly one point — normally at the switchboard or panel, not at the CT. Two grounds create a parallel path that steals current; no ground leaves the circuit floating at dangerous potential.
Never open an energized CT secondary. The CT is a current source; interrupting it drives the core into deep saturation and produces lethal voltage across the break. Short it first, at a test switch or with a shorting jumper on the terminal block, and verify the short before opening anything. See Safety Practices and CT & PT Fundamentals.

Two trip coils means two of everything. Transmission breakers usually have TC1 and TC2 fed from separate DC circuits and driven by separate relays or separate outputs, so no single failure disables tripping. When you commission one, prove each path independently — testing only TC1 and assuming TC2 works is how a redundant scheme quietly becomes a single point of failure. 5Conventions that trip people up

  • Shown de-energized. Every contact on a schematic is drawn in its state with no coil energized and the device in its reference condition. A normally-open contact of an operated relay is still drawn open.
  • “Normally” means the drawing state, not the usual operating state. A breaker that spends its life closed still has its 52a drawn open on a schematic that assumes an open breaker — check the drawing's stated reference condition.
  • Wire numbers versus terminal numbers. A wire number identifies the conductor across the whole station; a terminal number identifies a landing point on one block. Both appear, and they are not interchangeable.
  • Cross-reference notation. A coil on one sheet lists where its contacts appear, and each contact lists where its coil lives (typically as sheet/coordinate). Following these is how you trace a circuit across twenty drawings.
  • Dashed lines usually mean “by others” or “future.” Check the legend before assuming a dashed circuit exists.
  • Revision clouds and triangles mark what changed in the current revision. Read them first — they tell you what the last person found surprising.

6Red-lines and as-builts

The drawing set is only useful if it matches the station. Keeping that true is a discipline, not an accident:

  • Mark up as you go, in red, on the paper copy you carried in. Wire actually landed on TB4-7, not TB4-6. CT is 800:5, not 600:5. Spare pair used for the new alarm.
  • Photograph the red-lines before leaving site. Paper gets lost between the truck and the office.
  • Submit them. A red-line that never reaches the drafting group is a private note, and the next crew will believe the original drawing.
  • Note what you verified, not just what you changed. “Traced and confirmed as drawn” on a sheet is genuinely valuable information.
Treat drawing accuracy as part of the deliverable. On the automation side the equivalent artifact is the points list, and the same rule applies: the version in the field must equal the version in the archive. See FAT & SAT.

7Gotchas

  • The one-line rarely shows the actual switching state. It shows normal configuration. Ask operations what is actually open before planning work.
  • CT ratio on the drawing may be the CT's full ratio while the relay is connected to a tap. Multi-ratio CTs (e.g. 600:5 multi-tap) are extremely common, and the tap in use is a wiring fact, not a drawing fact — verify it.
  • Test switch drawings hide the shorting sequence. Know which blade shorts before it opens; the wrong sequence opens an energized CT.
  • Relay I/O numbering differs by model and by ordering option. A schematic drawn for one relay model does not transfer to a retrofit without re-checking every terminal.
  • Multiple drawing revisions in the same panel is normal on old stations. Find the latest, and be suspicious where two sheets disagree — that disagreement is exactly where the field is unknown.
  • Alarm contacts are often wired in series strings. One point in SCADA, six possible causes, and no way to tell which. Recognize this pattern on the elementary and push back at design time if the point matters.
