☰The rules, stated plainly

Fault current in six sentences

  • Most overhead faults are temporary. On distribution lines the large majority clear themselves once the arc is de-energized — which is the entire justification for reclosing.
  • Fault current is set by impedance, not by load. Between the source and the fault, sum the impedances; the current is what is left over. Load current is a rounding error by comparison.
  • The first cycle is not a sine wave. Inductive circuits force a decaying DC offset that can nearly double the first peak. Peak asymmetrical can approach 2.8 × the symmetrical RMS value on a very high X/R circuit.
  • Current decays. Generator contribution falls from subtransient through transient to steady state over several cycles to seconds; motor contribution disappears within a few cycles.
  • Arc and ground resistance are real and unhelpful. They reduce the current your relay sees, most severely for the ground faults you most want to detect.
  • The fault study is a settings input, not a formality. Maximum values set instantaneous pickups and equipment duty; minimum values set sensitivity and prove your protection reaches the far end.

1What a fault actually is

A fault is an unintended conducting path: phase to ground, phase to phase, or some combination. The insulation that was doing its job — air, oil, porcelain, polymer, or distance — stopped doing it.

The temporary/permanent split is why a distribution feeder relay recloses and a cable circuit generally does not: reclosing into a permanent cable fault buys nothing and destroys equipment. See Reclosing (79).

Faults evolve. A single-phase-to-ground arc heats and ionizes air; within a few cycles it can involve a second phase. An event report that starts as A–G and ends as A–B–G is completely ordinary, and it explains why an element that was correctly restrained at fault inception may operate a cycle later.

2The shape of the current in time

Close a switch onto an inductive short circuit and the current cannot jump — it must start from whatever it was. The solution is a steady-state sinusoid plus a decaying exponential sized to make the total start correctly. That exponential is the DC offset, and its time constant is τ = X / (ωR) = (X/R) / 377 seconds at 60 Hz.

The DC offset is what saturates CTs, what makes breaker “momentary” ratings different from “interrupting” ratings, and what a relay's digital filter is designed to reject. (Animated in the browser.)

The generator decrement trap. A synchronous generator's steady-state fault contribution can drop below its own rated current, so a plain overcurrent element may pick up and then drop out before timing out. This is why generator backup overcurrent is voltage-restrained or voltage-controlled (51V) — see Generator Protection.

3Where the current comes from

Fault current is a sum of contributions arriving from every direction that can supply it. That sum, not any single source, is what the breaker must interrupt — and the individual branches are what each relay sees.

The breaker interrupts the total; each relay measures a branch. Both numbers matter, and they are different numbers. (Animated in the browser.)

The high-impedance fault problem. A conductor lying on dry asphalt or gravel can draw only a few amperes — far below any pickup that would survive load. No conventional overcurrent element will ever see it, and no amount of sensitivity tuning changes that without tripping on load. Detection, where it exists, relies on arc signatures and harmonic content rather than magnitude, and it is a detection aid rather than a protection guarantee. Treat “we will catch downed conductors with a sensitive ground element” as a claim to be tested, not assumed.

5Reading a fault study

A short-circuit study returns, for each bus or line location, the current for each fault type under at least two system conditions. What you take from it:

Number you needWhich caseWhat it sets
Maximum three-phase at the relay busstrongest source, all generation onEquipment duty; the upper end of your instantaneous (50) pickup; CT saturation check
Maximum current for a fault just beyond the next devicestrongest sourceInstantaneous pickup must sit above this to avoid overreaching — typically with a 20–30% margin
Minimum fault at the far end of the protected zoneweakest source, contingency, and including fault resistanceSensitivity: the time-overcurrent (51) and ground pickups must reliably see this
Minimum SLG with assumed fault resistanceweakest sourceGround element pickup and, on lines, the resistive reach of distance elements
Through-fault at each downstream devicemaximumCoordination pairs: what both devices see simultaneously
X/R at the relay locationmaximum caseCT sizing and saturation assessment; breaker asymmetrical duty
The two-sided check. Every protective setting has to pass both directions: sensitive enough for the minimum credible fault it must detect, and secure enough for the maximum current it must ignore. If the two constraints cross — the minimum fault is below the maximum load, for instance — overcurrent alone cannot do the job, and you need a different quantity: negative sequence, distance, differential, or a communications-assisted scheme.

6Fault current and equipment duty

  • Interrupting rating — the symmetrical current a breaker can clear at contact-parting time, at a stated voltage. Modern ratings are symmetrical, with a separate multiplier for high X/R circuits.
  • Closing and latching (momentary) rating — the peak asymmetrical current the mechanism can survive if it closes into a fault. This is the number the DC offset attacks.
  • Short-time withstand — how long bus, cable, and CTs survive the current. Usually stated as a current for one or three seconds; scale with I²t.
  • CT saturation — the CT must reproduce the fault current, including its DC offset, long enough for the relay to decide. See CT & PT Fundamentals.
  • Arc-flash incident energy — driven by current and clearing time. Faster protection lowers incident energy, which is the entire premise of maintenance-mode settings and arc-flash detection. See Arc-Flash Detection.
Slower is not safer. Adding time delay for coordination raises incident energy at the same rate it improves selectivity. The two goals genuinely conflict, and the resolution is usually a scheme — a maintenance setting group, bus blocking, arc-flash light detection — not a compromise delay.

7Gotchas

  • Do not settle a study on the maximum case alone. Sensitivity failures — a far-end fault nobody trips for — are found in the minimum case, and they are the ones that burn conductors down.
  • Fault resistance is usually assumed zero in studies. Real ground faults are not. Ask what fault resistance was modeled before trusting a marginal sensitivity result.
  • Motor contribution is easy to forget on industrial and commercial buses, and it can add a meaningful percentage to first-cycle duty.
  • Inverter-based sources break classical assumptions. They may deliver barely more than rated current, and at a controlled angle rather than the network's natural fault angle — which can confuse directional and negative-sequence elements. Check the interconnection study, not a textbook. See DER Interconnection.
  • Measured never equals calculated. Arc resistance, source conditions at that instant, and pre-fault load all shift the number. Agreement within 10–20% on a phase fault is good; ground faults vary far more.
  • “Available fault current” on a nameplate is at the terminals. A few hundred feet of cable can cut it substantially — both good news for duty and bad news for sensitivity.
