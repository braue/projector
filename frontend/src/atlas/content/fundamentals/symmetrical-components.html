Symmetrical Components

The change of variables that makes unbalanced faults tractable — what positive, negative, and zero sequence physically are, and how every relay element uses them

☰The rules, stated plainly

What you actually need to hold

  • Any three phasors decompose into exactly three balanced sets: positive sequence (ABC, normal rotation), negative sequence (ACB, reversed), and zero sequence (all three identical, in phase).
  • Zero sequence means ground. 3I0 = IA + IB + IC is the residual current — the current that left the phases and came back through earth. No ground path, no I0.
  • Negative sequence means unbalance. I2 appears for any fault that is not three-phase, and for open conductors, blown fuses, and unbalanced load. It is nearly zero on a healthy balanced system, which makes it a superb, load-independent detector.
  • Positive sequence is the normal system. Load flows here. Any fault reduces V1 and raises I1.
  • Fault type has a signature: three-phase → only I1. Line-to-ground → I1 = I2 = I0. Line-to-line → I1 and I2, no I0. Line-to-line-to-ground → all three. This is how you read an event report.
  • Delta windings block zero sequence. A delta–wye transformer passes I1 and I2 (with a ±30° shift) but traps I0 in the delta — which is exactly why ground protection zones stop at transformers.

1The problem, and the trick

A balanced three-phase circuit can be solved one phase at a time; the other two follow by symmetry. A fault destroys that symmetry, and solving the resulting three coupled circuits by hand is unpleasant. In 1918 Fortescue showed that any set of three phasors is the sum of three balanced sets. Solve three easy balanced problems, add the answers, and you are done.

Writing a = 1∠120° (so a² = 1∠240° and 1 + a + a² = 0):

SequenceFrom phase quantitiesBack to phase A
Positive (1)I1 = ⅓(IA + a IB + a² IC)IA = I0 + I1 + I2
Negative (2)I2 = ⅓(IA + a² IB + a IC)
Zero (0)I0 = ⅓(IA + IB + IC)

Relays generally publish 3I0 and 3I2 rather than I0 and I2, because those are the quantities that appear directly in the circuit: 3I0 is literally the current in the neutral conductor, and using 3I2 puts negative-sequence pickups on the same numeric scale as phase currents.

Positive and negative sequence differ only in which way they rotate. Zero sequence does not rotate relative to itself at all — all three arrows move together. (Animated in the browser.)

2What each sequence physically is

Positive sequence — the system working Load current lives here. A motor fed with pure positive sequence produces forward torque. Every fault depresses V₁ near the fault and raises I₁. Distance elements and load-encroachment logic are positive-sequence concepts.
Negative sequence — the unbalance Reverse-rotating field. In a motor or generator it induces double-frequency rotor currents and heats the rotor — hence 46 thermal elements. On a feeder it flags anything asymmetric: single-phase faults, open conductors, blown fuses, single-pole operation, badly unbalanced single-phase load.
Zero sequence — the ground return Needs a physical return path: a grounded neutral, an earth return, or a delta winding to circulate in. Magnitude depends on how the system is grounded, which is why System Grounding changes ground-fault protection completely.
Why relays love negative sequence. On a balanced system I2 is essentially zero regardless of how heavily loaded the feeder is. That means a negative-sequence element can be set far more sensitively than a phase element without risking a load trip — it is a genuinely load-independent detector. The trade is that it sees nothing at all during a balanced three-phase fault, so it can never be your only protection.

3Sequence networks and how faults connect them

Each sequence gets its own single-phase equivalent circuit — a source behind an impedance. The three networks are independent except at the fault point, where the fault type dictates how they are wired together. That connection rule is the whole method.

Connection rules come from the boundary conditions at the fault. You rarely derive them — you recognize the resulting fingerprint. (Animated in the browser.)

Yes, an SLG fault can exceed a three-phase fault. When Z0 < Z1 — common right at the terminals of a solidly grounded delta–wye transformer — 3E/(Z1+Z2+Z0) beats E/Z1. Never assume the three-phase value bounds your duty; read the fault study for all four types.

Residual (3I0) versus neutral (IN) is a distinction worth being pedantic about. They should be equal, and when they differ the difference is CT error, a wiring mistake, or saturation. Relay settings name them differently (50G/51G for residual, 50N/51N for the measured neutral input on SEL feeder relays), and mixing them up produces an element that either never picks up or picks up on load.

6Reading sequence quantities off a real event

An SEL event report gives you the phase phasors and, in most models, the sequence quantities directly. The diagnosis sequence:

  1. Is 3I0 significant? If yes, ground is involved. If essentially zero, it is a phase fault or a balanced condition.
  2. Is 3I2 significant? If yes, the fault is unbalanced. If both 3I0 and 3I2 are near zero while current is high, it is a three-phase fault — or heavy balanced load.
  3. Compare magnitudes. I1 ≈ I2 ≈ I0 is a textbook single-line-to-ground fault. I1 ≈ I2 with no I0 is line-to-line.
  4. Check which phase. The faulted phase is the one whose current is elevated and whose voltage collapsed; sequence angles confirm it.
  5. Sanity-check against the fault study. Measured magnitude far from the study value means either a different fault location, arc resistance, or a CT/settings scaling problem.
A useful red flag: steady 3I0 present during normal operation. On a four-wire distribution feeder some residual is expected from unbalanced single-phase load. On a three-wire circuit it means a CT wiring error, a CT with a different ratio than its neighbors, or an actual standing ground condition. Chase it before it masks a real fault. 7Transformers and sequence networks

Transformer connection decides what each sequence sees, and this is what makes ground protection zone-bounded.

ConnectionPositive / negative sequenceZero sequence
Delta – wye (grounded)passes, with a ±30° shift (positive one way, negative the other)blocked through the transformer; the wye neutral is a zero-sequence source for the wye side, and the current circulates in the delta
Wye (grounded) – wye (grounded)passes, no shiftpasses — ground faults are visible from both sides, which complicates coordination
Delta – deltapasses, no shiftblocked both ways; neither side has a ground reference
Wye (ungrounded) – deltapassesblocked — no zero-sequence path at all
Two consequences you will meet constantly. First: a delta–wye transformer bounds the ground-fault zone, so feeder ground protection never has to coordinate with ground faults on the other side. Second: that ±30° shift is the reason a transformer differential relay needs connection compensation, and getting the compensation wrong produces a differential current that looks exactly like an internal fault — see Differential Protection (87) and Transformers.

8Gotchas

  • Phase rotation set wrong swaps sequence 1 and 2. A healthy system then shows large negative sequence. Check PHROT before believing any 46 or 67Q alarm on a newly commissioned relay.
  • 3I0 versus I0. Relay displays and settings are almost always in 3I0 and 3I2. Reading a study that reports I0 and setting the relay to that number gives you a pickup three times too sensitive.
  • Open-delta PTs cannot give you 3V0. No amount of settings work will produce a zero-sequence polarizing quantity from line-to-line voltages alone.
  • CT saturation manufactures false residual. A heavy through-fault that saturates one CT more than the others produces a large computed 3I0 with no ground fault present. This is why residual ground elements need a delay or a saturation-detection supervision on high-current buses.
  • Mutual coupling on parallel lines corrupts zero sequence. Zero-sequence current in one circuit induces zero sequence in the parallel circuit, which can push a zero-sequence directional element the wrong way. Negative-sequence polarizing does not suffer this.
  • Sequence quantities in a transformer's differential zone are compensated values, not raw measurements. Do not compare them against a study without accounting for the compensation.
