☰The rules, stated plainly

Integration

  • The points list is a contract between three parties — the device, the RTAC, and the master. All three copies must be one document, or the seams will drift.
  • Name by what it is, not by where it happens to be wired. A name that survives a relay replacement is a good name.
  • Collect generously, publish deliberately. Bring everything useful into the RTAC; expose to the master what the master needs. Data not collected is gone forever.
  • Quality must survive the trip. A stale value that looks healthy is the most dangerous thing on an operator's screen.
  • A control is not commissioned until it has operated the real device and the status came back. Both halves.
  • Alarms are a scarce resource. Every point promoted to an alarm makes every other alarm slightly less credible.

2Naming

A naming convention is worth having even if it is imperfect, because consistency beats cleverness. Common structure:

<station>_<equipment>_<quantity>[_<qualifier>]

NFLD_FDR1_52_Pos          breaker position
NFLD_FDR1_IA              phase A current
NFLD_FDR1_51P_Trip        time overcurrent trip
NFLD_XFMR1_TopOil_Temp    transformer top oil temperature
NFLD_XFMR1_LTC_TapPos     LTC tap position
  • Name the function, not the wiring. FDR1_52_Pos survives a relay replacement; RTAC_DI_17 does not.
  • Be consistent about abbreviations and write them down. Pos, Posn, and Position in one project is a search problem forever.
  • Do not encode the index in the name. Indices change; names should not.
  • The master's naming rules usually win at the northbound boundary. Get them in writing before you build the map, and record the mapping between your name and theirs.
  • Reserve a suffix scheme for control tags so a control and its status feedback are obviously related.

3What to map

Always Breaker and switch positions · trip and lockout status · per-phase currents and voltages · MW / MVAr · device self-test alarm · device communications status · control points for anything operable
Usually worth it Individual element operations (51P, 51G, 50P…) · reclose state and shot counter · ground current · frequency · power factor · demand values · active settings group · time-sync status
Collect but rarely publish Breaker wear and operation counters · through-fault accumulation · internal diagnostics · load profile · harmonics. These belong in a historian, not on an operator's screen
Do not map Anything that changes constantly with no operational meaning · duplicate representations of one fact · points nobody can name a use for. Every one of them costs bandwidth and attention
Collect southbound generously. The marginal cost of bringing an extra relay quantity into the RTAC is nearly zero, and it can be trended locally or published later without a site visit. The cost of not having collected it after a misoperation is a return trip and a gap in the record. Publish northbound deliberately — that side has real cost and real attention consequences.

4Analogs: scaling, units, deadbands

DecisionPractice
UnitsAgree primary units at the boundary and state them in the points list. Amperes primary, not secondary; kV not V; MW not W — unless the master's standard says otherwise
Where scaling happensPick one place and document it. Scaling in the relay and in the RTAC and at the master is how a value ends up a thousand times too large. See DNP3 on SEL Relays
Integer versus floatWhere the protocol offers both, float removes a whole class of scaling errors. Where it does not, record the divisor explicitly
DeadbandsLarge enough that normal noise does not generate events, small enough that real change is reported promptly. Set per point type, not globally — a bus voltage and a feeder current deserve different deadbands
Range and rolloverKnow the maximum the point can reach during a fault, not just during load. A 16-bit scaled analog that wraps at fault current is worse than useless
CountersDecide whether the master reads running totals or frozen counters, and whether rollover is handled at the RTAC or the master
Deadbands are the most common cause of “the SCADA is slow.” Too tight, and a handful of noisy analogs saturate the event queue so genuinely important events queue behind them. Too loose, and an operator watches a stale number. Set them from the actual measured noise on the live circuit, not from a default.

5Quality and the offline problem

The hardest integration question is not “what is the value?” but “should the operator believe it?”

  • Quality flags exist for this, and they must be carried all the way through: the device sets them, the RTAC tag carries them, and the northbound protocol reports them. A design that drops quality at any hop has broken the chain — see RTAC Tags & Quality.
  • Device offline is a first-class point. Every southbound device gets an explicit communications-status point published northbound. Without it, the master cannot distinguish a dead relay from a healthy relay reading zero.
  • Decide the offline behavior deliberately: hold last value with bad quality, or force to a defined state. Hold-last-value with good quality is the option that gets people hurt.
  • Group the status. One summary “station data invalid” point alongside the per-device detail gives the operator a fast answer and the engineer a precise one.
  • Test it. Pull a relay's Ethernet cable during commissioning and confirm the master shows the right thing within the expected time.

Test controls on an isolated breaker first. Prove the whole path — master click, protocol frame, RTAC tag, relay bit, output contact — on something that cannot cause an outage, then repeat on the real device during the outage window. The DNP3 comms checkout list covers the sequence.

7Alarm design

  • Not every status is an alarm. Separate indications (state the operator may want to see) from alarms (things requiring a response).
  • Prioritize honestly. If everything is high priority, nothing is. Three levels used consistently beats five used arbitrarily.
  • Summarize, then detail. One “transformer trouble” alarm with the individual causes available on demand beats forty individual alarms of equal weight — see Power Transformers.
  • Suppress the predictable flood. A device restart or a comms outage produces a burst of state changes; the design should collapse them rather than deliver hundreds of alarms.
  • Chattering points must be fixed, not tolerated. A contact that bounces produces thousands of events; use the relay's input debounce rather than filtering downstream.
  • Every alarm needs an action. If nobody can say what an operator should do about it, it is an indication, not an alarm.

8Point-to-point testing

This is the acceptance test for the whole integration, and there is no shortcut. For each point:

  1. Force the condition at the primary end — operate the contact, inject the current, move the switch. Not by forcing a bit in the RTAC.
  2. Observe it at each layer where practical: relay TAR, RTAC tag viewer, master screen.
  3. Confirm the value, the units, and the sign on analogs. A polarity error shows up here or during load checks, and nowhere else.
  4. Confirm the timestamp is sensible and carries true time on points that should have it.
  5. Record it, point by point, signed. “All points tested” without a per-point record is not a test result.
  6. Then test the failure cases: device offline, RTAC restart, redundant path failure.
Budget realistically. Point-to-point testing takes longer than anyone plans, especially when it needs a second person at the other end of a radio. Two hundred points at two minutes each is most of a day — and the two minutes assumes nothing is wrong.

9Change control

  • The points list is a published interface. Adding a point at the end is usually safe; renumbering existing indices breaks the master.
  • Never reuse an index for a different signal. If a point is retired, leave the index unused — the operator's historical trend still refers to it.
  • Coordinate with the control centre before northbound changes, and give them the updated list in whatever form they consume.
  • Version the points list alongside the RTAC project and the relay settings, so “what was live in March?” is answerable.
  • Re-test what you touched, and enough around it to be confident the map did not shift.

10Gotchas

  • Off-by-one indices. Zero-based versus one-based conventions differ between documents and tools. Verify by toggling a known point, not by reading.
  • Scaling applied twice. A value exactly 100 or 1000 times wrong is almost always this.
  • Class None points. A DNP3 point with no event class never reports spontaneously — it only appears on an integrity poll, so it looks like it works during testing and lags in service.
  • Duplicate server indices. Mapping the same index twice suppresses events on it.
  • A control that reports success and does nothing. Always confirm with the status point, and check the relay's own logic for what it does with the command.
  • Timestamps from the scan, not the source. Expected on Modbus, a mistake on DNP3 — check whether the events are actually carrying device time.
  • Points list maintained in three places. Nominate the authority and generate the rest.
  • No communications-status point for a device. The master cannot tell offline from zero, and eventually will not need to, because nobody will believe the screen anyway.
